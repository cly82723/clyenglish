Part A: the short task

What Part A asks

Part A is short, and that's why it's easy to lose marks. The best Part A answers aren't the most creative. They're the most complete.

Choice
None: everyone writes it
Length
About 200 words
You get
A role, a reader, notes to use
Key skill
Using every point given

The task puts you in a situation. You might be a club chairperson, a student helper or a class representative. You're given a reader and some information: notes, a poster, an email thread or a chat. Your job is to turn that information into the right kind of text, in the right voice, for the right reader.

  • Use every piece of information in the notes. A missing point is a missing mark.
  • Stay in role from the first line to the last.
  • Add a small, sensible detail to each point so it doesn't read like a copied list.
  • Writing 350 words. Extra length gives you more chances to make errors and leaves less time for Part B.
  • Copying the notes word for word. Rephrase them into full sentences.

Read the task like a marker

Before you write, answer four questions. Underline the answers on the question paper. Here is a practice task written for this guide:

Practice task. You are Chris Wong, chairperson of your school's English Club. The club is holding a Swap, Don't Shop second-hand book fair. Write an email to all students inviting them to donate books and come to the fair. Use the notes below.

Notes: Friday 15 March, 3:30–5:30 p.m., school hall · drop books at Room 204 before 13 March · one donated book = one swap token · leftover books go to a local charity.

Ask yourselfAnswer from the task
Who am I?Chris Wong, English Club chairperson: friendly but organised
Who am I writing to?All students: classmates, so a warm, semi-formal tone
Why am I writing?Two purposes: donate books and attend the fair
What must I include?Date, time, venue, drop-off place and deadline, token rule, charity

Most Part A answers that lose content marks answer only one of the two purposes. Count the verbs in the task. Each one is a job you must do.

Formats you must know

Format is part of your Organisation mark. Get the frame right, then spend your energy on the content.

Text typeOpeningClosingTone
EmailSubject line, then "Dear fellow students,""Best regards," + name + roleSemi-formal
Formal letter"Dear Mr Chan," / "Dear Sir or Madam,""Yours sincerely," / "Yours faithfully,"Formal
Blog postCatchy title, then a friendly first lineA question or invitation to commentPersonal, lively
ArticleHeadline, then a hookA memorable final thought; byline with your nameDepends on the publication
SpeechGreet the audience: "Good morning, Principal, teachers and fellow students.""Thank you for listening."Spoken, direct
  • "Yours sincerely" when you know the reader's name; "Yours faithfully" when you began "Dear Sir or Madam".
  • "Dear all students". Write "Dear fellow students" or "Dear schoolmates".

Plan in five minutes

Five minutes of planning saves you ten minutes of crossing out. Use this order every time.

  1. Tick the notesNumber every point you were given. You'll tick each one off as you use it.
  2. Group them into 3–4 paragraphsFor the practice task: purpose → how to donate → the fair day → why it matters.
  3. Write one opening lineSay who you are and why you're writing, in one sentence.
  4. Add one detail per paragraphA reason, a benefit, or a small example that the notes don't give you.
  5. Choose your closing actionWhat should the reader do next? Say it clearly in your last paragraph.

If you only have time for one thing: number the notes and tick them as you write. It is the simplest way to protect your Content mark.

From Level 3 to 5**

The opening of the practice email, written at four levels. Each one is correct English. What changes is how well it does the job.

Opening paragraph: the book fair email

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

I am Chris Wong. I am writing to tell you about the book fair. It will be held on 15 March in the school hall. Please come.

Complete but choppy: four short sentences in a row, and "Please come" gives no reason to come.

Level 4

I'm Chris Wong, chairperson of the English Club, and I'd like to invite you to our Swap, Don't Shop book fair on Friday 15 March in the school hall.

Sentences are joined, and the role is clear. Still only one of the two purposes: it forgets to ask for donations.

Level 5

Have you got books gathering dust on your shelf? The English Club needs them! On Friday 15 March, we're holding Swap, Don't Shop, a book fair where every book you donate earns you a token to take another one home.

A question hooks the reader, and both purposes, donate and attend, appear in the first paragraph.

Level 5**

Every bookshelf has one: the novel you finished in Form 2 and never opened again. On Friday 15 March, the English Club wants to give it a second life. Bring it to Swap, Don't Shop, trade it for something new to you, and help a local charity at the same time.

The reader recognises themselves in the first line. All three benefits (clear your shelf, get a new book, help a charity) arrive in three sentences, in a natural, confident voice.

Final check before you move on

Two minutes. Then go straight to Part B.

  • Every note is ticked off.
  • The format matches the text type: greeting, sign-off, subject line or headline.
  • Your name and role are where the task asked for them.
  • Verb tenses are right for future events: "will be held", "we're holding".
  • Using a real name when the task gives you a role name. Use the name in the task.

Part A is a warm-up for your brain. Finish it with confidence, then give Part B your best time.
