Part B: the long task

Choosing your question

You get four questions and answer one. Choose the question you can develop, not the topic you like most.

A topic you love but can only say two things about gives you a thin answer. A plain topic where you can see three strong points scores higher. Spend three minutes choosing: it is the most important decision in the paper.

  1. Cross out text types you haven't practisedIf you have never written a report, the exam is not the place to try.
  2. For the rest, list three points in 30 secondsCan't find three? Cross it out.
  3. Check every part of the questionMany questions have two or three parts ("describe… explain… suggest…"). You must answer all of them.
  4. Pick the one with the strongest third pointYour third idea is usually where the best answers pull ahead.

How each level chooses

3"I like sport, so I'll do the sports question."

5"I can find three points for the sports question."

5**"I'll take the proposal: I have three points, and I know its format by heart."

CLY: Format you already know is free Organisation marks, so it counts when you choose.

The 10-minute plan

Ten minutes of planning saves twenty minutes of rewriting. Use the same four moves every time.

  1. Minutes 0–2: decode the questionWrite down the text type, the reader and the purpose. Underline every task verb (describe, explain, suggest).
  2. Minutes 2–5: find ideasAsk who is affected (students, parents, the school, society) and when (now, later). Write five ideas; keep the best three.
  3. Minutes 5–8: CREI notesFor each idea, a few words each for the Claim, Reason, Example (make it local) and Impact. See CREI paragraphs.
  4. Minutes 8–10: first and last linesWrite your opening and your closing sentence in full, so you know where the piece is going.

The content method

Content marks reward ideas that are relevant, developed and right for the reader. Four moves get you there, in every task.

  1. Find ideas: who and whenAsk who is affected (students, parents, the school or employers, society) and when (now, and later). Each box in the grid is a possible idea.
  2. Choose threeKeep the three ideas that are most different from each other, and that you can give a real example for.
  3. Develop each one: why, how, so whatWhy is it true? How does it work in real life? So what: why should this reader care?
  4. Make it localGive every idea a Hong Kong setting: a district, a habit, a place the reader knows.

Develop one idea: why, how, so what

3 The idea, stated

Opening facilities is good for health.

True, but it could be written about any city, by anyone, without thinking.

5 The idea, with a reason

Opening facilities is good for health because many residents in crowded districts have nowhere to exercise after work.

"Why" is answered. "How" and "so what" are still missing.

5** Why, how and so what, in a Hong Kong setting

In districts like Sham Shui Po, where many families live in small flats and public courts are often full, a school basketball court open until nine would be the only free space many residents have to exercise. Why (no space), how (a court open until nine), so what (the city pays twice), and a real district the reader can picture.

Past-paper tasks, reviewed

Eight real Part B tasks. For each: the ideas, lines from real sample answers, and the same idea taken up to 5**.

The 2024 and 2025 cards quote real candidates from the HKEAA's official exemplars, marked by examiners (from the HKEAA website). The older cards quote tutors' published samples, so treat their levels as the tutors' claims.

Letter to the editor · 2024 Q4

Hong Kong now has many more areas such as parks, shops and restaurants that welcome dogs. Some people feel that the city has gone too far in its efforts to be dog-friendly. The Hong Kong Times is asking readers for their opinion. Write a letter to the editor expressing whether or not you support this new trend.

The ideas

  • For: owners can bring dogs along instead of leaving them at home; dogs as company; a more open, modern city.
  • Against: many people are afraid of dogs; a crowded city has no room for them; restaurants and malls lack the facilities.
  • Use the question's words: "gone too far" invites a middle position: support the trend, but not everywhere.

From the official exemplars: the same idea at two levels

Both scripts argue against the trend, and both start from the same idea: some people are afraid of dogs.

3 "…some of the people living in Hong Kong are scared of dogs. If most of the areas in Hong Kong become dogs-friendly, it will definitately affort people's life."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3 (the candidate's spelling is kept)

5 "It is not a rare experience for me to encounter startled pedestrians as I walk my dogs on streets, indicating that a significant proportion of people are not comfortable with dogs."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3

What changed: the Level 3 writer states the idea and imagines a scene. The Level 5 writer is a dog owner arguing against dog-friendly places, so their own experience becomes evidence the reader trusts, and "indicating that" turns one walk into a conclusion. The Level 5 letter also ends on a line worth borrowing: "the city itself cannot be dog-friendly. Only we humans can."

One step further: the same idea at 5**

5** Experience, a local setting, and a sharper impact

Even as a dog owner, I have lost count of the pedestrians who step into the road when my two dogs come towards them on a narrow Mong Kok pavement. They are not unkind or unreasonable; they are frightened, and in a city where a pavement is often barely wide enough for two people, they have nowhere else to go. A trend that asks them to share restaurants and buses with dogs as well has not made the city friendlier. It has simply decided whose comfort counts.

It keeps the Level 5 writer's best move (a dog owner's own experience), adds a place the reader can picture, and ends with an impact that reframes the whole debate as a question of fairness.

Essay · 2025 Q4

Discuss the challenges of group work in the workplace and provide suggestions for improvement. Write an essay about this.

The ideas

  • Challenges: clashing schedules and working styles; slow decisions and endless meetings; nobody feeling responsible; conflict between members.
  • Suggestions: one named owner per task; clear deadlines; shared online tools; short regular check-ins.
  • Pair them: every suggestion should solve a challenge you have already described. A list of tips that answers nothing loses Content marks.

From the official exemplars: the same idea at two levels

Both scripts open with the same challenge: team members work at different times.

3 "One of the major challenges of group work in the workplace is that employees have different working schedule."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3 (the candidate's grammar is kept)

5 "For example, a member who prefers working at night would find it difficult to agree on a meeting time with another member who likes working during the day."

5 "When tasked as a group, individuals tend to feel less responsible for the task, as they can easily push the responsibility onto another member."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3

What changed: the Level 3 writer names the challenge. The Level 5 writer shows it with two specific people, then finds a deeper challenge most candidates miss: when a task belongs to everyone, nobody feels responsible for it.

From a published 5** sample

"When one individual falls behind, the entire group bears the consequences."

Sample on PL Learning

What works: one short sentence carries a whole cause and effect, which makes a strong topic sentence. The same sample pairs each challenge with a practical fix, such as named roles, fixed deadlines and shared planning tools.

One step further: the same idea at 5**

5** The Level 5 idea, named, shown and solved

The quietest problem in any team is the one no one owns. When a task belongs to five people, each can assume that someone else will chase the supplier or check the figures, and on the morning of the deadline the team discovers that everyone assumed. Psychologists call this diffusion of responsibility; managers simply call it a missed deadline. Naming one owner for every task, however small, costs nothing and removes the place where work goes to hide.

A memorable claim, a scene from a real office, the proper name for the effect, and a suggestion that answers exactly this challenge.

Letter to the editor · 2016 Q2

Because of a lack of sports facilities, some people have suggested that schools open their sports facilities to the public after school hours. Write a letter to the editor of the Hong Kong Daily stating your opinion, supported by three reasons.

The ideas

  • For: residents' health; making use of idle public property; closer ties between school and neighbourhood.
  • The other side: security, cleaning and wear; students losing practice time. Answer it with a booking system and set hours.

From a published sample

"…promoting healthier lifestyles, fostering social cohesion, and maximizing the use of existing resources."

Sample answer 2, Icecream Tutor

What works: the three reasons come from three different angles (the person, the community, the city's money), so they never overlap. What to add: each abstract noun ("cohesion", "resources") needs a scene or a place to prove it.

One paragraph, three levels: using idle property

3 Claim, fact, claim again

Secondly, schools have many sports facilities. They are not used at night. So schools should open them.

No reason why it matters, no example, and it ends where it started.

5 A reason and a point about public money

Secondly, opening school facilities would make better use of public money. Most school halls and courts sit empty after six, even though they were built with taxpayers' money. Letting residents use them would turn an idle space into a community asset.

Logical and complete. The example is still general: which courts, which residents?

5** A sharper claim, a visible contrast, and the "how"

Secondly, the city already owns the facilities it is short of; it simply locks them at six. Every evening, hundreds of school courts stand empty while public pitches nearby are booked out. Opening them with a simple booking system and an extra caretaker shift would add sports space without pouring a single new foundation.

The claim turns the problem around ("we already own them"). The example is a contrast you can see. The impact answers "how", which quietly deals with the cost objection.

Article · 2015 Q4

Many parents in Hong Kong send their children overseas for schooling. Write an article for your school magazine examining two reasons for this, and discussing its impact on local schools and on the children who leave to study overseas.

The ideas

  • Two reasons: a pull (a wider, international education) and a push (exam pressure and competition at home).
  • Impact, part 1: local schools lose students, classes and subject choices.
  • Impact, part 2: the children gain independence but can lose family time and their place in the local system.
  • Forgetting a part. This task has three: reasons, impact on schools, impact on children.

From a published sample

Reason 1: an international curriculum and a multicultural setting. Reason 2: heavy academic pressure and fierce competition in local schools.

Summary of the sample on Icecream Tutor

What works: one reason pulls families abroad and the other pushes them out, so the two reasons explain the trend from both sides. What to add: the impact paragraphs are where marks are won, because most answers spend all their words on the reasons.

One paragraph, three levels: the impact on local schools

3 A judgement, not an impact

This trend is bad for local schools because many students leave.

"Bad" how? The reader learns nothing they didn't already know.

5 A chain of consequences

This trend affects local schools too. As more families leave, some schools have fewer students and fewer classes, which means less funding and fewer subject choices for those who stay.

Cause → effect → effect. Clear, but still told from a distance.

5** The chain, made concrete

The students who leave are not the only ones affected. When a class of thirty loses five students to boarding schools abroad, the school does not just lose five names from a register; it may lose a class, a teacher's post and the elective that only ran because enough students chose it. The cost of one family's decision is quietly paid by the classmates left behind.

The same chain, shown with numbers and things a reader can picture. The last sentence turns the impact into a point about fairness.

Debate speech · 2018 Q5

You are representing your school at an inter-school debating competition. The motion is: "The policy of reporting students' class position should be abolished." Write a debate speech arguing for or against the motion.

The ideas

  • For: rankings damage motivation; they label students; they push learning towards marks instead of skills.
  • Against: rankings prepare students for competition; they give parents clear information; they reward effort.
  • Either side: answer the other team's strongest point, as the published sample does in its first paragraph.

From a published sample

"Examination is only a means to test our knowledge and learn from mistakes."

"This division has a labelling effect that stigmatizes students by putting them into categories."

Wendy Chan, 2018 DSE Writing Question 5 (sample)

What works: the first line goes back to what exams are for, which makes ranking look like a misuse of them. The second names a precise effect, labelling, instead of saying rankings are "unfair". What to add: a speech should sound spoken: address the judges, use a short scene, end lines with rhythm.

One paragraph, three levels: motivation

3 A feeling, not an argument

Firstly, class position is bad for students. It makes them sad and they do not want to study.

It could be true, but nothing explains how or proves it.

5 The mechanism, explained

Firstly, class position reporting damages motivation. When a report card says "35th out of 36", a student stops seeing exams as a way to learn and starts seeing them as proof that they have failed.

A concrete detail ("35th out of 36") and a clear chain of cause and effect. It still reads like an essay.

5** The same argument, written for the ear

Honourable judges, ask yourselves what "35th out of 36" teaches a fifteen-year-old. Not which topics to revise, not which mistakes to fix: only that thirty-four classmates did better. An exam should tell a student where to go next; a ranking only tells them where to stand. Abolish the ranking, and we give the exam back its purpose.

It speaks to the judges, uses a pattern you can hear ("not… not… only"), and ends on a balanced line built on the sample's best idea.

Proposal · 2016 Q7

The Hong Kong Youth Association is organising a Youth Festival to showcase popular youth trends in Hong Kong. Write a proposal suggesting one or two trends popular with young people, how they could be presented at the Festival, and why.

The ideas

  • Pick trends you can show, not just describe: street art, sustainable fashion, e-sports, cafés and latte art, hiking.
  • For each: what, how, why. What the trend is, how visitors experience it at the Festival, and why it deserves the space.
  • Describing the trend for 300 words and leaving "how to present it" to the last line.

From a published sample

"To showcase this trend at the festival, we propose setting up a dedicated 'Art Alley'."

Sample on Icecream Tutor

What works: "propose" plus a named, concrete plan. A proposal is judged on whether the reader can picture it happening. What to add: answer the practical questions (space, cost, permission) before the reader asks them.

One paragraph, three levels: presenting street art

3 A wish

We suggest street art. Many young people like it. We can let them draw on the wall.

Which wall? Who draws? Whose permission? A decision-maker can't approve this.

5 A plan with a reason

We propose an "Art Alley" where local street artists paint murals live during the Festival. Visitors could watch, ask questions and add their own designs to a community wall, so they take part in the trend instead of just looking at it.

What, who and why are answered. The practical problems are still open.

5** A plan that answers the objections

We propose turning the Festival's entrance corridor into an "Art Alley": six plywood panels, each painted live by a local street artist over the weekend, with the final panel left blank for visitors. Painting on removable boards avoids the obvious problem, permission to paint public walls, and the finished panels could tour community centres afterwards, so the trend outlives the Festival.

Where, how many, who, the objection solved, and a benefit that lasts. Every sentence makes approval easier.

Report · 2017 Q7

You are working on a project called "Hong Kong's NEETs": young people not in education, employment or training, many of whom spend their time at home playing video games or surfing the internet. Write a report explaining why the number of NEETs is rising and suggesting what can be done to help them, with reasons.

The ideas

  • Causes: overprotective parenting; neglect and lack of support in poorer families; a hard first step into work.
  • Help: social workers and support groups; subsidised part-time jobs as a first step; funding for further study.
  • Match them: each recommendation should answer one cause.

From a published sample

"…they fail to develop self-care ability and problem-solving skill and become overly dependent on their parents."

"Starting with a small step by taking a part-time job, they may feel easier to get on with work"

Wendy Chan, 2017 DSE Writing Question 7 (sample)

What works: the sample finds causes at both extremes, rich families and poor ones, which is a mature, balanced idea. Its recommendations are small, realistic steps. What to add: a report needs evidence under each finding (figures, interviews), and recommendations clearly linked back to findings.

One paragraph, three levels: a finding

3 A list of opinions

There are many reasons why NEETs are increasing. One reason is parents. Some parents spoil their children.

"Many reasons" and "spoil" are vague, and a report needs findings, not opinions.

5 A cause, explained

One cause is overprotective parenting. In some well-off families, parents handle every problem for their children, so the young people never learn to cope with difficulties on their own. When they face pressure at school or work, they simply withdraw.

A clear chain from cause to result, in a report's neutral voice. No evidence yet.

5** Finding, evidence, and the link to a recommendation

Finding 1: overprotection, not laziness. Social workers interviewed for this project described the same pattern: young people whose parents had chosen their schools, packed their bags and settled their arguments for them. Having never faced a problem alone, many found their first setback at work too much to bear, and retreated to the one place where nothing was expected of them. This suggests that support should build independence, not simply find jobs.

A heading that corrects a common assumption, evidence from the project, and a last line that leads straight into the recommendations. (The interviews are invented for practice; in the exam, invent sensible ones.)

Story · 2022 Q8

A competition called "Stories of Survival" asks for a story on the theme "Nature: friend or enemy?". Write a story about a hiker caught in a storm in the mountains.

The ideas

  • Answer the theme: decide early whether nature turns out to be friend, enemy or both, and build towards that turn.
  • Use a real place: Lion Rock, Tai Mo Shan or the MacLehose Trail gives you details for free.
  • Five paragraphs of weather. The storm is the setting; the story is what the hiker decides.

From a published sample

The sample opens on a hiker named Emily in a mountain valley as the weather turns, then a sudden downpour turns the trail to mud.

Summary of the sample on Icecream Tutor

What works: it gets the hiker into the storm quickly. What to add: "the weather turns" is how most scripts begin. A 5** opening shows the storm through one small, specific detail.

One paragraph, three levels: the storm arrives

3 Telling the feeling

Suddenly, it rained very heavily. I was very scared. I did not know what to do.

"Suddenly", "very" twice, and the feeling is named instead of shown.

5 Showing the danger

Within minutes the drizzle became a downpour. The path turned to mud under my boots, and the mist swallowed the trail markers one by one. I realised I had no idea which way led down.

Scene and feeling are shown through what the hiker sees. The turn ("no idea which way") raises the stakes.

5** Scene, feeling, turn, and the theme

The first drop hit the map in my hand, and by the time I had folded it, the ridge behind me had disappeared. Rain drummed on my hood so loudly that I could no longer hear my own breathing. I counted the orange ribbons tied to the trees: three, then two, then none. The mountain had not changed; it had simply stopped showing me the way.

Small details carry the fear (the map, the ribbons counting down), and the last line answers the theme: nature is not cruel here, just indifferent.

What every 5** paragraph above has in common

3States the idea and stops.

5Explains why, with a general example.

5**Explains why and how, proves it with a scene or a number, and says why it matters.

Level ladders

One line from each text type, rewritten at Level 3, 5 and 5**. Read the reason under each level: that is the move to copy.

Opening line: "Teenagers and stress" for a school magazine

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Nowadays, many students are very stressed. There are many reasons for this problem.

"Nowadays" and "many reasons" appear in thousands of scripts. The reader learns nothing new.

Level 5

Ask any S5 student what keeps them awake at night, and the answer is rarely just exams.

It talks to the reader directly and sets up a surprise for the next sentence: what else is it?

Level 5**

It is 11:47 p.m. Kelly has finished her homework, her tutorial worksheet and half a past paper. She is still not sure it is enough.

A tiny scene shows stress instead of naming it. Short sentences create pressure. The final line turns one student's night into the article's question.

Answering the other side: plastic bag levy

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Some people think the levy is bad for poor people. But I think it is good.

It mentions the other side but never answers it.

Level 5

Critics argue that a higher levy would burden low-income families. However, a reusable bag costs less than a month of levies, so the change saves money over time.

The objection is stated fairly and answered with a reason, not just disagreement.

Level 5**

The concern for low-income families is fair, and it is exactly why the levy should come with free reusable bags at public housing estates. A good policy does not ask the poor to pay for a cleaner harbour; it makes the cleaner choice the cheaper one.

It agrees with part of the objection and turns it into a better proposal. The last sentence is balanced and quotable.

Call to action: joining a volunteer programme

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

So I hope you will join the programme. Thank you.

Polite, but nothing tells the audience how or when.

Level 5

Sign-up forms are at the General Office until Friday. Bring a friend, and you'll both see how much one Saturday morning can change.

A concrete step, a deadline, and a reason to act now.

Level 5**

One Saturday. Three hours. One elderly neighbour who finally has someone to talk to. The forms are at the General Office until Friday. The only question is whether you'll be there.

Fragments build rhythm you can hear. The ending returns the choice to the listener, which is what a strong speech does.

"The plan" section: a reading corner

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

We want to make a reading corner. It will have many books and sofas. Students will like it.

A wish, not a plan. Where? Who pays? Who looks after it?

Level 5

We propose converting the unused corner of the covered playground into a reading area with two bookshelves and floor cushions. Books will be donated by students, and Library Prefects will manage the area at lunchtime.

Place, materials, source and staffing are all answered.

Level 5**

The corner beside the covered playground is used only for storing PE mats, which can move to the gym. With two shelves and floor cushions (about $3,000 from the Student Union budget) and books donated through a class competition, it could open within a month and be staffed at lunchtime by Library Prefects, at no cost to teachers.

It solves the objections before the principal raises them: space, cost, timing and staff time. The cost figure is part of a practice task, so invent a reasonable one in the exam.

A finding: the school canteen survey

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Many students don't like the canteen food because it is not healthy and too expensive.

"Many" is vague, and two separate findings are squeezed into one sentence.

Level 5

Of the 120 students surveyed, nearly two-thirds said they found the canteen's lunch sets too oily. Price was a smaller concern, raised by about a quarter.

Numbers replace "many", and the two issues are ranked.

Taste was not the problem: most respondents rated the food as tasty. The main complaint was oiliness, mentioned by nearly two in three students, which suggests that changing cooking methods, not the menu, would make the biggest difference.

It reads the data instead of just listing it, and the finding leads naturally to the recommendation. (The survey figures are for practice; in the exam, invent sensible ones.)

Showing a feeling: nervous before a performance

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

I was very very nervous before I went on stage.

It tells the feeling. "Very very" is a sign the writer needed a stronger image.

Level 5

My hands were shaking so badly that I nearly dropped my violin as I waited behind the curtain.

The body shows the nerves. The reader sees it happening.

CREI paragraphs

Every body paragraph in an article, letter, speech or proposal can follow four moves. Claim, Reason, Evidence or Example, Impact.

  • CClaim One sentence: your point, answering the question directly.
  • RReason Why it is true: the logic, often with because, since or so.
  • EEvidence / Example Proof: a fact, a figure or a scene, ideally from Hong Kong.
  • IImpact So what? The consequence for the reader, and the link back to your stance.

Reports and stories use their own patterns; see Text-type features.

One paragraph, three levels

Practice task: should secondary schools start at 9 a.m. instead of 8 a.m.?

3 Claim and a vague example, then the claim again

CFirstly, schools should start later. EFor example, many students sleep very late because they have a lot of homework. CSo schools should start later.

No reason, no impact. The last sentence repeats the first, so the paragraph goes in a circle.

5 All four moves, in order

CFirst, a later start would improve students' health. RTeenagers' body clocks naturally run later, so asking them to be alert at 8 a.m. works against their biology. EMany S5 students in my school sleep after midnight because of homework and tutorial classes, yet leave home before 7. IAs a result, they arrive tired and learn less in their first lessons.

Complete and logical. The example is real but general, and the impact stops at "learn less".

5** All four moves, each one sharpened

CThe strongest case for a later start is not comfort but learning. RSleep research shows that a teenager's body clock runs up to two hours later than an adult's, so an 8 a.m. lesson asks students to think at the hour their brains are least ready. EWalk into any S5 classroom at 8:15 and the evidence is in front of you: heads on desks, a quiz half-finished, a teacher repeating instructions for the third time. IIf the first two lessons of every day are lost to fatigue, we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

The claim answers the obvious objection ("it's just about comfort"). The example is a scene you can see. The impact turns tiredness into a cost everyone cares about.

Each paragraph below is jumbled. Tap C, R, E or I for every sentence, then check. A wrong label gets a hint, not the answer.

Task: should phones be allowed in lessons?

  1. When my English teacher let us check collocations on our phones during a writing lesson, the whole class corrected mistakes we had been making all term.

  2. Schools should allow phones in lessons, but only as learning tools.

  3. Banning phones outright would throw away a tool that, used well, makes students more independent learners.

  4. Used with clear rules, a phone gives every student instant access to dictionaries, news and practice tools that a textbook cannot match.

  1. A small charge changes habits because people notice the cost every single time they pay it.

  2. Applying the same idea to containers could cut one of the largest sources of waste in our landfills.

  3. The government should extend the plastic levy to all takeaway containers.

  4. After Hong Kong's plastic bag levy began, carrying a reusable bag quickly became normal at the supermarket checkout.

A whole letter, analysed

The plan from Step 0, written out as a letter to the editor (about 400 words). Read each paragraph, then the note under it.

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to your article "Should the school bell ring later?" (12 March). As an S5 student who leaves home at 6:45 every morning, I strongly support moving the start of the secondary school day to 9 a.m., and I believe the benefits far outweigh the costs.

Opening. Why I'm writing (the article and its date), why I'm worth listening to (an S5 student at 6:45), and my stance in one sentence. No "Firstly" yet: the reader knows where the letter is going.

CThe strongest case for a later start is not comfort but learning. RSleep research shows that a teenager's body clock runs up to two hours later than an adult's, so an 8 a.m. lesson asks students to think at the hour their brains are least ready. EWalk into any S5 classroom at 8:15 and the evidence is in front of you: heads on desks, a quiz half-finished, a teacher repeating instructions for the third time. IIf the first two lessons of every day are lost to fatigue, we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

Body 1, CREI. The strongest argument goes first. "Not comfort but learning" answers the obvious objection before anyone raises it.

CA later start would also protect students' mental health. RSleep loss does not just make teenagers tired; it makes them more anxious and less able to cope with pressure. EMany of my classmates finish homework and tutorial worksheets after midnight, then sleep barely six hours before the next school day begins. IAn extra hour each morning will not remove exam pressure, but it would give students the rest they need to face it.

Body 2, CREI. "Also" links it to the first point. The Impact admits a limit ("will not remove exam pressure"), which makes the claim more believable, not less.

Admittedly, critics worry that a later start would clash with parents' working hours and push extracurricular activities into the evening. These concerns are real, but they are practical problems with practical solutions. Schools could open their libraries from 7:30 for students who must arrive early, and some activities could move to lunchtime or one free afternoon a week.

The other side. Concede ("Admittedly… These concerns are real"), then answer with solutions, not just disagreement. This paragraph is what most Level 4 letters are missing.

I therefore urge the Education Bureau to invite a small group of secondary schools to trial a 9 a.m. start for one year, and to publish the results on attendance, grades and well-being. If the evidence is as clear as I expect, Hong Kong students could start each day ready to learn, rather than simply present.

Closing. A recommendation that is specific (who, what, how long, how to measure it) and a last line that echoes the learning argument from Body 1.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

The closing, three levels

3In conclusion, I think schools should start later. Thank you.

5I hope the government will consider letting schools start at 9 a.m.

5**I therefore urge the Education Bureau to invite a small group of schools to trial a 9 a.m. start for one year…

CLY: A letter is organised when every paragraph has one job and the reader could name it.

Text-type features

Each text type has its own reader, register and frame. Get the frame right, then spend your energy on the content.

Article

An article is written for readers of a magazine, newsletter or website. It has to earn their attention from the headline onwards.

Reader
Magazine or newsletter readers
Register
Lively, but well organised
Format
Headline · byline · paragraphs
Paragraphs
CREI
  1. HeadlineShort and specific. A question, a pun or a bold claim.
  2. Hook paragraphA scene, a surprising fact or a question the reader can't ignore.
  3. Two or three CREI body paragraphsOne idea each, with an example from Hong Kong life.
  4. EndingReturn to your opening image, or leave the reader with something to do.
  • Give your article a headline. Many students forget, and it's part of the format.
  • Use subheadings only if the publication would. A website article, yes; a short magazine piece, rarely.
  • "In conclusion," at the end of an article. It sounds like an essay. End with an image or a call to action.

Phrases worth borrowing

  • Picture this: …
  • It's a familiar story: …
  • What most people don't realise is that …
  • The numbers tell only half the story.
  • So, what can we actually do?
  • Next time you …, remember …

Letter to the editor

You're writing to a newspaper to argue a point in public. The editor is your addressee, but your real audience is every reader of that newspaper.

Reader
The public, through the editor
Register
Formal and reasoned
Format
"Dear Editor," … "Yours faithfully,"
Paragraphs
CREI
  1. Why you're writingRefer to the article or issue and state your view in one sentence.
  2. Argument one (CREI)Your strongest point, with evidence or an example.
  3. Argument two (CREI)A different angle: cost, fairness, health, the environment.
  4. The other sideAdmit one opposing point, then answer it.
  5. What should happenA clear, realistic recommendation.
  • "Yours faithfully," then a name. The task may give you a pen name such as Chris Wong; use it.
  • Use formal linking: "Furthermore", "Admittedly", "It follows that".
  • Contractions (don't, it's) in a formal letter.

See a whole letter, analysed paragraph by paragraph: A whole letter, analysed.

Speech

A speech is writing for the ear. If it doesn't sound good read aloud, it isn't finished.

Reader
A live audience
Register
Direct, warm, persuasive
Format
Greeting · body · thanks
Paragraphs
CREI, with signposts
  1. Greeting"Good morning, Principal, teachers and fellow students."
  2. Introduce yourself and the topicWho you are and why you're speaking today.
  3. Signpost"I'd like to share three reasons…" Listeners can't flip back a page.
  4. Body (CREI)One point at a time, with a story or a question to the audience.
  5. Call to action and thanksTell them exactly what to do, then "Thank you."
  • Use "you" and "we" often. Ask the audience questions.
  • Use the rule of three: three examples, three adjectives, three short sentences.
  • Writing a speech that reads like an essay with a greeting stuck on top.

Proposal

A proposal asks someone with power, such as a principal or a committee, to approve a plan. It wins by being practical, not passionate.

Reader
A decision-maker
Register
Formal and businesslike
Format
To · From · Date · Subject, then headings
Paragraphs
CREI under each heading
  1. HeaderTo: The Principal · From: Chris Wong, Student Union · Date · Subject: Proposal for a reading corner
  2. IntroductionThe purpose of the proposal, in one or two sentences.
  3. BackgroundThe problem now, with evidence: a survey, an observation.
  4. The planWhat, where, when, who, and what it costs.
  5. Benefits (CREI)Who gains and how.
  6. ConclusionA polite request for approval.
  • Use headings. A busy principal should be able to skim them.
  • Use "would" and "could" to sound reasonable: "This would allow…"
  • Emotional pleading: "Please, please approve this, students really need it!"

Report

A report tells a reader what was found and what should be done. Facts first, opinions last, and clearly labelled.

Reader
Someone who asked for it
Register
Formal, objective
Format
Title or memo header, headings
Paragraphs
Finding → evidence → recommendation
  1. Title or header"Report on Students' Use of the School Canteen"
  2. IntroductionWho asked for the report, its purpose, and how information was collected.
  3. FindingsOne heading per area: the finding, then the evidence (figures, comments). Past tense, mostly.
  4. RecommendationsWhat should change, each one linked to a finding.
  5. ConclusionA one-paragraph summary.
  • Use the passive where it sounds natural: "A survey was conducted…", "It was found that…"
  • Keep opinions in the Recommendations section.
  • Writing "I think" in the Findings section.

Story

A story is judged on how well you control the reader's experience. Show what happened; don't just report it.

Reader
Usually a competition or magazine
Register
Vivid, controlled
Format
Title, paragraphs, dialogue
Paragraphs
Scene → feeling → turn
  1. Start in the middleBegin with action or dialogue, not "One day…"
  2. Build tensionEach paragraph: a scene, the feeling it causes, and a turn that moves the story on.
  3. The turning pointThe moment the character changes or learns something.
  4. A short endingLink back to the question's prompt or the first line.
  • Keep your tenses consistent: tell the story in the past tense.
  • Punctuate dialogue correctly: "Ready?" she whispered.
  • Ending with "…and then I woke up. It was all a dream."

Sentence patterns

Patterns that fit each text type. Use two or three in a piece, never all of them.

Patterns marked "From a real script" come from candidates in the HKEAA's official 2024 and 2025 exemplars. For linking words and sentence variety that suit any task, see the Toolkit.

Article

  • Picture this: …

    Picture this: it is 11 p.m., and an S5 student is opening her third tutorial worksheet of the day.

    A hook that puts the reader inside a scene.

  • It is easy to blame … . The real problem is …

    It is easy to blame phones for our poor sleep. The real problem is what we are scrolling away from.

    Corrects a common view, so your article has something new to say.

  • What most people don't realise is that …

    What most people don't realise is that many tutorial centres are busiest on Sunday mornings.

    Signals a surprising fact or angle.

  • Question? Answer.

    Why do so many students give up reading for pleasure? Because nobody has time to read anything that isn't tested.

    A question the reader is already asking, answered at once.

  • The numbers tell only half the story.

    Visitor numbers are rising. The numbers tell only half the story: most visitors stay for less than a day.

    Moves from statistics to their meaning.

  • Next time you …, remember …

    Next time you order a drink with a plastic lid, remember that the lid will outlive you.

    An ending that sends the reader away with something to do.

Letter to the editor

  • I am writing in response to …

    I am writing in response to your article "Should the school bell ring later?" (12 March).

    The standard opening: name what you are answering.

  • While I understand …, I firmly believe …

    While I understand the concerns of parents, I firmly believe that a later start would benefit every student.

    Shows balance and states your stance in one sentence.

  • Admittedly, … . However, …

    Admittedly, a later start would affect parents' working hours. However, schools could open their libraries early for students who must arrive first.

    The concession-and-reply move that most Level 4 letters are missing.

  • Adjective + as + subject + be, … From a real Level 4 script, 2024 Q4

    Divergent as the arguments are, it is my firm conviction that the new trend should be supported.

    A concession built into one sentence.

  • Coupled with …, … only … From a real Level 5 script, 2024 Q4

    Coupled with the crowded nature of our city, the negative effects of welcoming dogs only compound in magnitude.

    Adds a second factor that makes the first worse. (One verb corrected from the script.)

  • X is not …, nor is Y … . From a real Level 5 script, 2024 Q4

    "Dog-friendly" is not a buzzword, nor is welcoming dogs merely a friendly gesture.

    Rejects two shallow views before giving your own.

  • I therefore urge … to …

    A closing recommendation with a clear actor and action.

    Good morning, Principal, teachers and fellow students.

    Greet every group in the room, most senior first.

    Pulls listeners in; a reader can't flip back, so you must keep them with you.

  • Rule of three

    It costs us our sleep, our health and our love of learning.

    Three items in a row are easy to hear and remember.

  • Imagine …

    Imagine a school where every student finishes the day with time to spare.

    Turns a proposal into a picture.

  • Not because …, but because …

    We should volunteer, not because it looks good on a portfolio, but because someone is waiting for us.

    Corrects a weak reason with a stronger one; it sounds good aloud.

  • So today, I ask you to …

    So today, I ask you to sign up before Friday, and bring a friend.

    The call to action: say exactly what to do.

    The purpose of this proposal is to suggest a reading corner in the covered playground.

    The opening line of the introduction.

  • This would allow … to …

    "Would" makes benefits sound reasonable, not pushy.

  • The estimated cost is …, which could be covered by …

    Answers "how much" before the reader asks.

    An inverted conditional: formal and confident.

  • We would be grateful if you could …

    A polite, specific request to end on.

    States the purpose and scope in the first line.

    The passive keeps the focus on the method, not on you.

  • It was found that …

    It was found that most respondents considered the lunch sets too oily.

    Introduces a finding objectively.

  • The majority of …, while only …

    The majority of respondents rated the food as tasty, while only a quarter mentioned price.

    Compares two findings and ranks them.

  • In light of these findings, …

    In light of these findings, the following changes are recommended.

    The bridge from findings to recommendations.

  • It is recommended that … (should) + base verb

    It is recommended that the canteen (should) offer at least one steamed option each day.

    Formal recommendation; keep "I think" out of a report.

Story

  • Open with dialogue or action From a real Level 5 script, 2025 Q2

    "Brother, brother," my younger sister, Mary, pulled on my sleeve, "I want to ride that."

    Starts in the middle, not with "One day…".

  • Show a feeling through the body

    My hands were shaking so badly that I nearly dropped my violin.

    The reader sees the nerves instead of being told about them.

  • Short sentence. Then a fragment.

    The lights went out. Silence. Then a scream from the back row.

    Short pieces speed the reader up at moments of tension.

  • No sooner had … than …

    No sooner had I stepped onto the ridge than the rain began.

    Inversion that makes two events feel almost simultaneous.

  • The moment I …, I knew …

    The moment I saw his empty desk, I knew something had changed.

    Marks the turning point clearly.

  • An ending that echoes the beginning

    Opening: "I want to ride that." Ending: this time, I was the one pulling her sleeve.

    Returning to the first image shows the change in the character.

Answering the other side, three levels

3Some people say dogs are dirty. But I think it is OK.

5Admittedly, some owners do not clean up after their dogs. However, most follow the rules.

5**Divergent as the views are, the solution is not to ban dogs but to fine the few owners who spoil it for the rest.
