Part B: the long task

Choosing your question

You get eight questions and answer one. Choose the question you can develop, not the topic you like most.

A topic you love but can only say two things about will give you a thin answer. A plain topic where you can see four strong points will score higher. Spend three minutes choosing. It is the most important decision in the paper.

  1. Cross out text types you haven't practisedIf you have never written a report, the exam is not the place to try.
  2. For the rest, list three points in 30 secondsCan't find three? Cross it out.
  3. Check every part of the questionMany questions have two or three parts ("describe… explain… suggest…"). You must answer all of them.
  4. Pick the one with the strongest third pointYour third idea is usually where the best answers pull ahead.
  • Choose a text type you know the format for. Format is free Organisation marks.
  • Changing your mind after writing a paragraph. Trust your plan.

Article

An article is written for readers of a magazine, newsletter or website. It has to earn their attention from the headline onwards.

Reader
Magazine or newsletter readers
Register
Lively, but well organised
Format
Headline · byline · paragraphs
Signature move
A hook in the first line

Structure

  1. HeadlineShort and specific. A question, a pun or a bold claim.
  2. Hook paragraphA scene, a surprising fact or a question the reader can't ignore.
  3. Two or three body paragraphsOne idea each, with an example from Hong Kong life.
  4. EndingReturn to your opening image, or leave the reader with something to do.

Opening line: "Teenagers and stress" for a school magazine

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Nowadays, many students are very stressed. There are many reasons for this problem.

"Nowadays" and "many reasons" appear in thousands of scripts. The reader learns nothing new.

Level 4

Stress has become a part of daily life for many Hong Kong students, who face long school days, tutorial classes and high expectations.

Specific causes replace "many reasons." The setting is Hong Kong, not anywhere.

Level 5

Ask any S5 student what keeps them awake at night, and the answer is rarely just exams.

It talks to the reader directly and sets up a surprise for the next sentence: what else is it?

Level 5**

It is 11:47 p.m. Kelly has finished her homework, her tutorial worksheet and half a past paper. She is still not sure it is enough.

A tiny scene shows stress instead of naming it. Short sentences create pressure. The final line turns one student's night into the article's question.

  • Give your article a headline. Many students forget, and it's part of the format.
  • Use subheadings only if the publication would. A website article, yes; a short magazine piece, rarely.
  • "In conclusion," at the end of an article. It sounds like an essay. End with an image or a call to action.

Phrases worth borrowing

  • Picture this: …
  • It's a familiar story: …
  • What most people don't realise is that …
  • The numbers tell only half the story.
  • So, what can we actually do?
  • Next time you …, remember …

Letter to the editor

You're writing to a newspaper to argue a point in public. The editor is your addressee, but your real audience is every reader of that newspaper.

Reader
The public, through the editor
Register
Formal and reasoned
Format
"Dear Editor," … "Yours faithfully,"
Signature move
A clear stance from paragraph one

Structure

  1. Why you're writingRefer to the article or issue and state your view in one sentence.
  2. Argument oneYour strongest point, with evidence or an example.
  3. Argument twoA different angle: cost, fairness, health, the environment.
  4. The other sideAdmit one opposing point, then answer it.
  5. What should happenA clear, realistic recommendation.

Answering the other side: plastic bag levy

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Some people think the levy is bad for poor people. But I think it is good.

It mentions the other side but never answers it.

Level 5

Critics argue that a higher levy would burden low-income families. However, a reusable bag costs less than a month of levies, so the change saves money over time.

The objection is stated fairly and answered with a reason, not just disagreement.

Level 5**

The concern for low-income families is fair, and it is exactly why the levy should come with free reusable bags at public housing estates. A good policy does not ask the poor to pay for a cleaner harbour; it makes the cleaner choice the cheaper one.

It agrees with part of the objection and turns it into a better proposal. The last sentence is balanced and quotable.

  • "Yours faithfully," then a name. The task may give you a pen name such as Chris Wong; use it.
  • Use formal linking: "Furthermore", "Admittedly", "It follows that".
  • Contractions (don't, it's) in a formal letter.

Speech

A speech is writing for the ear. If it doesn't sound good read aloud, it isn't finished.

Reader
A live audience
Register
Direct, warm, persuasive
Format
Greeting · body · thanks
Signature move
Talking to the audience

Structure

  1. Greeting"Good morning, Principal, teachers and fellow students."
  2. Introduce yourself and the topicWho you are and why you're speaking today.
  3. Signpost"I'd like to share three reasons…" Listeners can't flip back a page.
  4. BodyOne point at a time, with a story or a question to the audience.
  5. Call to action and thanksTell them exactly what to do, then "Thank you."

Call to action: joining a volunteer programme

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

So I hope you will join the programme. Thank you.

Polite, but nothing tells the audience how or when.

Level 5

Sign-up forms are at the General Office until Friday. Bring a friend, and you'll both see how much one Saturday morning can change.

A concrete step, a deadline, and a reason to act now.

Level 5**

One Saturday. Three hours. One elderly neighbour who finally has someone to talk to. The forms are at the General Office until Friday. The only question is whether you'll be there.

Fragments build rhythm you can hear. The ending returns the choice to the listener, which is what a strong speech does.

  • Use "you" and "we" often. Ask the audience questions.
  • Use the rule of three: three examples, three adjectives, three short sentences.
  • Writing a speech that reads like an essay with a greeting stuck on top.

Proposal

A proposal asks someone with power, such as a principal or a committee, to approve a plan. It wins by being practical, not passionate.

Reader
A decision-maker
Register
Formal and businesslike
Format
To · From · Date · Subject, then headings
Signature move
Answer "how" and "how much"

Structure

  1. HeaderTo: The Principal · From: Chris Wong, Student Union · Date · Subject: Proposal for a reading corner
  2. IntroductionThe purpose of the proposal, in one or two sentences.
  3. BackgroundThe problem now, with evidence: a survey, an observation.
  4. The planWhat, where, when, who, and what it costs.
  5. BenefitsWho gains and how.
  6. ConclusionA polite request for approval.

"The plan" section: a reading corner

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

We want to make a reading corner. It will have many books and sofas. Students will like it.

A wish, not a plan. Where? Who pays? Who looks after it?

Level 5

We propose converting the unused corner of the covered playground into a reading area with two bookshelves and floor cushions. Books will be donated by students, and Library Prefects will manage the area at lunchtime.

Place, materials, source and staffing are all answered.

Level 5**

The corner beside the covered playground is used only for storing PE mats, which can move to the gym. With two shelves and floor cushions (about $3,000 from the Student Union budget) and books donated through a class competition, it could open within a month and be staffed at lunchtime by Library Prefects, at no cost to teachers.

It solves the objections before the principal raises them: space, cost, timing and staff time. The cost figure is part of a practice task, so invent a reasonable one in the exam.

  • Use headings. A busy principal should be able to skim them.
  • Use "would" and "could" to sound reasonable: "This would allow…"
  • Emotional pleading: "Please, please approve this, students really need it!"

Report

A report tells a reader what was found and what should be done. Facts first, opinions last, and clearly labelled.

Reader
Someone who asked for it
Register
Formal, objective
Format
Title or memo header, headings
Signature move
Findings → recommendations

Structure

  1. Title or header"Report on Students' Use of the School Canteen"
  2. IntroductionWho asked for the report, its purpose, and how information was collected.
  3. FindingsWhat you found, one heading per area. Past tense, mostly.
  4. RecommendationsWhat should change, linked to each finding.
  5. ConclusionA one-paragraph summary.

A finding: the school canteen survey

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Many students don't like the canteen food because it is not healthy and too expensive.

"Many" is vague, and two separate findings are squeezed into one sentence.

Level 5

Of the 120 students surveyed, nearly two-thirds said they found the canteen's lunch sets too oily. Price was a smaller concern, raised by about a quarter.

Numbers replace "many", and the two issues are ranked.

Level 5**

Taste was not the problem: most respondents rated the food as tasty. The main complaint was oiliness, mentioned by nearly two in three students, which suggests that changing cooking methods, not the menu, would make the biggest difference.

It reads the data instead of just listing it, and the finding leads naturally to the recommendation. (The survey figures are for practice; in the exam, invent sensible ones.)

  • Use the passive where it sounds natural: "A survey was conducted…", "It was found that…"
  • Keep opinions in the Recommendations section.
  • Writing "I think" in the Findings section.

Story

A story is judged on how well you control the reader's experience. Show what happened; don't just report it.

Reader
Usually a competition or magazine
Register
Vivid, controlled
Format
Title, paragraphs, dialogue
Signature move
One turning point

Structure

  1. Start in the middleBegin with action or dialogue, not "One day…"
  2. Build tensionSomething goes wrong or a choice appears.
  3. The turning pointThe moment the character changes or learns something.
  4. A short endingLink back to the question's prompt or the first line.

Showing a feeling: nervous before a performance

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

I was very very nervous before I went on stage.

It tells the feeling. "Very very" is a sign the writer needed a stronger image.

Level 5

My hands were shaking so badly that I nearly dropped my violin as I waited behind the curtain.

The body shows the nerves. The reader sees it happening.

Level 5**

Behind the curtain, I counted the bow hairs. Two hundred and something. I lost count every time the audience laughed at the act before mine.

The word "nervous" never appears. A strange, specific action reveals the feeling, and the reader trusts the writer completely.

  • Keep your tenses consistent: tell the story in the past tense.
  • Punctuate dialogue correctly: "Ready?" she whispered.
  • Ending with "…and then I woke up. It was all a dream."

Show, don't tell

5** Top scripts sound like a real person with a real view. Write for your reader, not for the marker.
