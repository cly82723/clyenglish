Part B: the long task

Five steps, from choosing a question to the last five minutes. Tap one to open it.

1

Step 1 · Choose and planChoose the question you can find three points for. Then plan for ten minutes.

Weaker: "I like sport, so I'll do the sports question." Stronger: "I'll take the proposal: I have three points, and I know its format by heart."

Lesson 1 of 2

Choose the question

You get four questions and answer one. Choose the question you can develop, not the topic you like most.

A topic you love but can only say two things about gives you a thin answer. A plain topic where you can see three strong points scores higher. Spend three minutes choosing: it is the most important decision in the paper.

  1. Cross out text types you haven't practisedIf you have never written a report, the exam is not the place to try.
  2. For the rest, list three points in 30 secondsCan't find three? Cross it out.
  3. Check every part of the questionMany questions have two or three parts ("describe… explain… suggest…"). You must answer all of them.
  4. Pick the one with the strongest third pointYour third idea is usually where the best answers pull ahead.

1 The example

Choosing from the 2025 paper

One student gives each question 30 seconds and writes down every point that comes to mind.

Set a timer for two minutes. Give each question 30 seconds and write down every point you can, then choose one.

  • Q2 · Essay: a century ago Hong Kong women had five children on average; now it is fewer than one. Why is a smaller family now seen as better?
  • Q3 · Blog post: you are a musician. A reader asks how to get people to listen to a song they recorded themselves.
  • Q4 · Letter to the editor: Hong Kong has many more dog-friendly parks, shops and restaurants. Has it gone too far?

Before you commit, tick each line that is true of the question you chose.

One student's choice

Q4 (letter to the editor). Three different points came quickly: people who are afraid of dogs, pavements too narrow to share, and restaurants and malls without the space or bins. The dog owners' view gives an other side to answer, and the format is one practised all year.

Q2 had points too (the cost of housing, careers, more freedom), but all of them were general. With no Hong Kong examples to hand, every paragraph would have stayed vague.

Q3 and Q5 depend on experience the student doesn't have, so every detail would have been invented.

3 Why this works

Why three points beat a favourite topic

Content marks reward development, not interest. Three points you can explain fill about 400 words. A topic you love with only two things to say runs out by the third paragraph, and the marker sees it.

A format you know is free Organisation marks. The salutation, the headings, the sign-off: none of them cost you thought, so all your attention goes on ideas.

CLY: Format you already know is free Organisation marks, so it counts when you choose.

Lesson 2 of 2

Plan in ten minutes

Ten minutes of planning saves twenty minutes of rewriting. Use the same four moves every time.

  1. Minutes 0–2: decode the questionWrite down the text type, the reader and the purpose. Underline every task verb (describe, explain, suggest).
  2. Minutes 2–5: find ideasAsk who is affected (students, parents, the school, society) and when (now, later). Write five ideas; keep the best three.
  3. Minutes 5–8: notes for each ideaA few words each for four moves: the Claim (your point), the Reason (why it is true), the Evidence (a Hong Kong example) and the Impact (why it matters). Step 3 teaches these four moves in full.
  4. Minutes 8–10: first and last linesWrite your opening and your closing sentence in full, so you know where the piece is going.

3 Why this works

Why ten minutes of planning pays

Decoding first stops the biggest loss: an answer that misses part of the question cannot reach the top Content band, however well it is written.

Notes for every move mean each paragraph already has its reason and its example before you start, so you write instead of thinking mid-sentence.

Writing the last line while you plan means every paragraph is heading somewhere. Most weak endings were never planned.

Next: Step 2 · Content

2

Step 2 · ContentUse three different ideas, and give each one a Hong Kong example.

Weaker: For example, many people want to do exercise. Stronger: In districts like Sham Shui Po, where public courts are often full, a school court open until nine…

Content marks reward ideas that are relevant, developed and right for the reader. Four moves get you there, in every task.

  1. Find ideas: who and whenAsk who is affected (students, parents, the school or employers, society) and when (now, and later). Each box in the grid is a possible idea.
  2. Choose threeKeep the three ideas that are most different from each other, and that you can give a real example for.
  3. Develop each one: why, how, so whatWhy is it true? How does it work in real life? So what: why should this reader care?
  4. Make it localGive every idea a Hong Kong setting: a district, a habit, a place the reader knows.

1 The example

The grid, filled in

Task (2016 Q2): should schools open their sports facilities to the public after school hours?

WhoNowLater
ResidentsSomewhere to exercise after workHealthier habits, fewer health problems
StudentsLess space for their own practice?Neighbours who know and value the school
The schoolSecurity and cleaning costsStronger ties with the community
SocietyIdle public property put to useLower medical costs for the city

The best three are the most different from each other: health (residents), using idle property (society), and community ties (the school). The costs box becomes your answer to the other side.

Develop one idea: why, how, so what

3 The idea, stated

Opening facilities is good for health.

True, but it could be written about any city, by anyone, without thinking.

5 The idea, with a reason

Opening facilities is good for health because many residents in crowded districts have nowhere to exercise after work.

"Why" is answered. "How" and "so what" are still missing.

5** Why, how and so what, in a Hong Kong setting

In districts like Sham Shui Po, where many families live in small flats and public courts are often full, a school basketball court open until nine would be the only free space many residents have to exercise. A city that keeps its courts locked at night is paying for its people's poor health twice: once to build the courts, and again in hospital bills.

Why (no space), how (a court open until nine), so what (the city pays twice), and a real district the reader can picture.

3 Why this works

Why different, local ideas score

Markers reward ideas that are different, not ideas that are many. Three points about the same group of people read like one point said three times. The who-and-when grid forces you to look at the question from somewhere else.

Developing an idea is what lifts it a level. "Why" makes it logical, "how" makes it real, and "so what" tells the reader why they should care.

A local example is proof. A district, a habit or a number the reader recognises shows you are writing about Hong Kong, not reciting a general truth.

Next: Step 3 · Organisation

3

Step 3 · OrganisationBuild every body paragraph in four moves: Claim, Reason, Evidence, Impact.

CA later start would improve students' health. RTeenagers' body clocks run later. EMany S5 students sleep after midnight, yet leave home before 7. IThey arrive tired and learn less.
  • CClaim Your point, in one sentence.
  • RReason Why it is true.
  • EEvidence Proof: a fact, a figure or a scene from Hong Kong.
  • IImpact Why it matters to the reader.

1 The example

Should secondary schools start at 9 a.m. instead of 8 a.m.?

One paragraph, three levels

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

CFirstly, schools should start later. EFor example, many students sleep very late because they have a lot of homework. CSo schools should start later.

No reason and no impact. The last sentence repeats the first.

Level 5

CFirst, a later start would improve students' health. RTeenagers' body clocks naturally run later, so asking them to be alert at 8 a.m. works against their biology. EMany S5 students in my school sleep after midnight because of homework and tutorial classes, yet leave home before 7. IAs a result, they arrive tired and learn less in their first lessons.

All four moves, in order. The example is real but general.

Level 5**

CThe strongest case for a later start is not comfort but learning. RSleep research shows that a teenager's body clock runs up to two hours later than an adult's, so an 8 a.m. lesson asks students to think at the hour their brains are least ready. EWalk into any S5 classroom at 8:15 and the evidence is in front of you: heads on desks, a quiz half-finished, a teacher repeating instructions for the third time. IIf the first two lessons of every day are lost to fatigue, we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

All four moves, each one sharper. The example is a scene you can see.

2 Try it

Practise: label each sentence

Each paragraph below is jumbled. Tap C, R, E or I for every sentence, then check. A wrong label gets a hint, not the answer. There are five sets of two paragraphs.

Task: should phones be allowed in lessons?

  1. When my English teacher let us check collocations on our phones during a writing lesson, the whole class corrected mistakes we had been making all term.

  2. Schools should allow phones in lessons, but only as learning tools.

  3. Banning phones outright would throw away a tool that, used well, makes students more independent learners.

  4. Used with clear rules, a phone gives every student instant access to dictionaries, news and practice tools that a textbook cannot match.

Task: should the plastic levy cover takeaway containers?

  1. A small charge changes habits because people notice the cost every single time they pay it.

  2. Applying the same idea to containers could cut one of the largest sources of waste in our landfills.

  3. The government should extend the plastic levy to all takeaway containers.

  4. After Hong Kong's plastic bag levy began, carrying a reusable bag quickly became normal at the supermarket checkout.

Task: should schools keep school uniforms?

  1. At my school, students who used to be teased for cheap trainers now look exactly like everyone else.

  2. Keeping uniforms protects students from a quiet form of bullying that money would otherwise decide.

  3. Schools should keep school uniforms.

  4. A uniform removes the daily pressure to compete over expensive clothes and brands.

Task: should the government build more cycling tracks?

  1. Safe tracks make cycling a realistic way to make short journeys instead of taking a car.

  2. The government should build more cycling tracks in the new towns.

  3. More tracks would cut traffic and give residents a cheap way to stay healthy.

  4. In Sha Tin, the riverside track is packed every weekend with families and commuters on bikes.

Task: should schools set homework at weekends?

  1. A homework-free weekend would send students back on Monday ready to learn rather than simply tired.

  2. Students need unbroken time to rest; otherwise they start each week already exhausted.

  3. Many S5 students spend their Sundays finishing worksheets instead of seeing their families.

  4. Schools should not set homework at weekends.

Task: should Hong Kong host more international sports events?

  1. Hong Kong should host more international sports events.

  2. During the Hong Kong Sevens, the restaurants near the stadium are full all weekend.

  3. Major events attract visitors who spend money in hotels, restaurants and shops.

  4. Hosting more events would boost the economy and give young athletes role models to look up to.

Task: should secondary schools teach cooking?

  1. Cooking is a basic life skill that helps young people eat more healthily.

  2. Teaching cooking now would give students the independence they will need the day they leave home.

  3. Secondary schools should teach students how to cook.

  4. When my cousin moved into university halls, she lived on instant noodles because she had never learnt to cook.

Task: should fast-food restaurants show calorie counts?

  1. When a burger shop near my school started showing calories on its menu, many of my classmates switched to smaller meals.

  2. Fast-food restaurants should display calorie counts on their menus.

  3. Clear labels would help the city tackle obesity without banning any food.

  4. People make better choices when they can see what they are about to eat.

Task: should every student do community service?

  1. Every secondary student should do community service.

  2. Community service would turn students into citizens who notice the needs around them.

  3. After volunteering at a food bank in Kwun Tong, my class started a monthly food drive of its own.

  4. Helping others teaches responsibility in a way that classroom lessons cannot.

Task: should some public libraries open 24 hours a day?

  1. Students who live in small flats often end up studying in fast-food restaurants late at night.

  2. Many students have no quiet space to study at home in the evening.

  3. Some public libraries should open 24 hours a day.

  4. Round-the-clock libraries would give these students a safe, free place to work.

Check your own paragraph

3 Why this works

Why the 5** paragraph scores higher

The Level 3 paragraph goes in a circle. It states the claim, gives a vague example, and states the claim again. A marker learns nothing new after the first sentence.

The Level 5 paragraph is complete and logical. Its impact stops at "learn less", which is true of any tired student anywhere.

The Level 5** paragraph answers the obvious objection first: that a later start is only about comfort. Its impact turns tiredness into a cost everyone cares about.

Reports and stories do not use CREI. They have their own patterns: see Text types.

The last sentence, three levels

3So schools should start later.

5As a result, they arrive tired and learn less in their first lessons.

5**…we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

4

Step 4 · LanguageReplace vague words with precise ones.

Weaker: Mobile phones are bad for students. Stronger: Mobile phones distract students in class.

Language marks reward words that are accurate and exact, not words that are long. Swap every vague word for the one that says exactly what you mean.

  1. Find the vague wordUsually good, bad, many, things, very or a lot.
  2. Ask the reader's next questionBad how? How many? Which things?
  3. Answer it in the sentenceAn exact verb, a number, or the things themselves, named.
  4. Check it is still correctA plain word used correctly beats a rare word used wrongly.

1 The example

One sentence, three levels

3 The vague word

Mobile phones are bad for students.

Bad how? The reader has to guess.

5 One exact verb

Mobile phones distract students in class.

"Distract" answers "bad how?" in one word.

5** The exact word, made into a scene

A phone buzzing under the desk can pull a student out of a lesson three or four times an hour.

The reader can see it, and can check the number against their own school day.

VagueAskExact
goodGood how?affordable · convenient · reliable
badBad how?harmful · distracting · overcrowded
manyHow many?over half · three in five · hundreds of
thingsWhich things?name them: rules, fees, opening hours
very importantWhy?say why: "…because the landfills are almost full"

Check your own draft

3 Why this works

Why exact words score

Language marks reward accuracy and range, not rare words. "Distract" is an everyday word, but it does a job that "bad" cannot.

An exact word answers the reader's next question before they ask it. A vague word leaves them to fill the gap, and markers do not fill gaps for you.

A stronger word is not a more exact word. "Terrible" and "huge" only shout louder; they still don't say how, or how many.

CLY: If you could swap your word for "good" or "bad" and the sentence still made sense, it isn't exact yet.

5

Step 5 · Put it togetherFollow your text type's format, and answer every part of the question.

A proposal needs To, From, Date and Subject, then headings.

Keep the last five minutes for one read-through. Check what costs the most marks: every part of the question, then the format.

  1. Every part of the questionFind each task verb you underlined. Point to the paragraph that answers it.
  2. The formatThe opening, the sign-off, and the headings your text type needs.
  3. Your own three slipsThe mistakes you make most: verb endings, tenses, articles. Look for those, not for everything.

1 The example

Five minutes on one letter

Practice task: write a letter to the editor giving your view on a 9 a.m. start, and suggest one change that would make it work. One student's read-through found four things.

FoundFixed
No paragraph answers "suggest one change"Two sentences added before the last line: open the library from 7:30 for students whose parents leave early.
"Dear Editor," … "Yours sincerely,""Yours faithfully," for a reader you do not know by name.
Paragraph 3 ends "So schools should start later."The last line now says what is lost: the first two lessons of every day.
"Many student is tired""Many students are tired": this student's most common slip, so it was the one looked for.

3 Why this works

Why the last five minutes go on these

A missing part limits your Content mark more than any weak sentence. It is also the quickest thing to fix: two sentences.

Format is Organisation marks you already earned by practising. Losing them to a wrong sign-off wastes them.

Looking for your own slips finds more than reading for "anything wrong". Markers notice patterns, so fixing one repeated slip lifts the whole answer.
