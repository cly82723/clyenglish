Part B: the long task

Choosing your question

You get four questions and answer one. Choose the question you can develop, not the topic you like most.

A topic you love but can only say two things about gives you a thin answer. A plain topic where you can see three strong points scores higher. Spend three minutes choosing: it is the most important decision in the paper.

  1. Cross out text types you haven't practisedIf you have never written a report, the exam is not the place to try.
  2. For the rest, list three points in 30 secondsCan't find three? Cross it out.
  3. Check every part of the questionMany questions have two or three parts ("describe… explain… suggest…"). You must answer all of them.
  4. Pick the one with the strongest third pointYour third idea is usually where the best answers pull ahead.

How each level chooses

3"I like sport, so I'll do the sports question."

5"I can find three points for the sports question."

5**"I'll take the proposal: I have three points, and I know its format by heart."

CLY: Format you already know is free Organisation marks, so it counts when you choose.

The 10-minute plan

Ten minutes of planning saves twenty minutes of rewriting. Use the same four moves every time.

  1. Minutes 0–2: decode the questionWrite down the text type, the reader and the purpose. Underline every task verb (describe, explain, suggest).
  2. Minutes 2–5: find ideasAsk who is affected (students, parents, the school, society) and when (now, later). Write five ideas; keep the best three.
  3. Minutes 5–8: CREI notesFor each idea, a few words each for the Claim, Reason, Example (make it local) and Impact. See CREI paragraphs.
  4. Minutes 8–10: first and last linesWrite your opening and your closing sentence in full, so you know where the piece is going.

Level ladders

One line from each text type, rewritten at Level 3, 5 and 5**. Read the reason under each level: that is the move to copy.

Opening line: "Teenagers and stress" for a school magazine

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Nowadays, many students are very stressed. There are many reasons for this problem.

"Nowadays" and "many reasons" appear in thousands of scripts. The reader learns nothing new.

Level 5

Ask any S5 student what keeps them awake at night, and the answer is rarely just exams.

It talks to the reader directly and sets up a surprise for the next sentence: what else is it?

Level 5**

It is 11:47 p.m. Kelly has finished her homework, her tutorial worksheet and half a past paper. She is still not sure it is enough.

A tiny scene shows stress instead of naming it. Short sentences create pressure. The final line turns one student's night into the article's question.

Answering the other side: plastic bag levy

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Some people think the levy is bad for poor people. But I think it is good.

It mentions the other side but never answers it.

Level 5

Critics argue that a higher levy would burden low-income families. However, a reusable bag costs less than a month of levies, so the change saves money over time.

The objection is stated fairly and answered with a reason, not just disagreement.

Level 5**

The concern for low-income families is fair, and it is exactly why the levy should come with free reusable bags at public housing estates. A good policy does not ask the poor to pay for a cleaner harbour; it makes the cleaner choice the cheaper one.

It agrees with part of the objection and turns it into a better proposal. The last sentence is balanced and quotable.

Call to action: joining a volunteer programme

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

So I hope you will join the programme. Thank you.

Polite, but nothing tells the audience how or when.

Level 5

Sign-up forms are at the General Office until Friday. Bring a friend, and you'll both see how much one Saturday morning can change.

A concrete step, a deadline, and a reason to act now.

Level 5**

One Saturday. Three hours. One elderly neighbour who finally has someone to talk to. The forms are at the General Office until Friday. The only question is whether you'll be there.

Fragments build rhythm you can hear. The ending returns the choice to the listener, which is what a strong speech does.

"The plan" section: a reading corner

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

We want to make a reading corner. It will have many books and sofas. Students will like it.

A wish, not a plan. Where? Who pays? Who looks after it?

Level 5

We propose converting the unused corner of the covered playground into a reading area with two bookshelves and floor cushions. Books will be donated by students, and Library Prefects will manage the area at lunchtime.

Place, materials, source and staffing are all answered.

Level 5**

The corner beside the covered playground is used only for storing PE mats, which can move to the gym. With two shelves and floor cushions (about $3,000 from the Student Union budget) and books donated through a class competition, it could open within a month and be staffed at lunchtime by Library Prefects, at no cost to teachers.

It solves the objections before the principal raises them: space, cost, timing and staff time. The cost figure is part of a practice task, so invent a reasonable one in the exam.

A finding: the school canteen survey

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

Many students don't like the canteen food because it is not healthy and too expensive.

"Many" is vague, and two separate findings are squeezed into one sentence.

Level 5

Of the 120 students surveyed, nearly two-thirds said they found the canteen's lunch sets too oily. Price was a smaller concern, raised by about a quarter.

Numbers replace "many", and the two issues are ranked.

Level 5**

Taste was not the problem: most respondents rated the food as tasty. The main complaint was oiliness, mentioned by nearly two in three students, which suggests that changing cooking methods, not the menu, would make the biggest difference.

It reads the data instead of just listing it, and the finding leads naturally to the recommendation. (The survey figures are for practice; in the exam, invent sensible ones.)

Showing a feeling: nervous before a performance

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

I was very very nervous before I went on stage.

It tells the feeling. "Very very" is a sign the writer needed a stronger image.

Level 5

My hands were shaking so badly that I nearly dropped my violin as I waited behind the curtain.

The body shows the nerves. The reader sees it happening.

Level 5**

Behind the curtain, I counted the bow hairs. Two hundred and something. I lost count every time the audience laughed at the act before mine.

The word "nervous" never appears. A strange, specific action reveals the feeling, and the reader trusts the writer completely.

What changes at each level

3Correct, but could be about anyone, anywhere.

5Specific: real causes, real numbers, a real next step.

5**Specific and shaped: a scene, a turn or a solution the reader didn't expect.

CREI paragraphs

Every body paragraph in an article, letter, speech or proposal can follow four moves. Claim, Reason, Evidence or Example, Impact.

  • CClaim One sentence: your point, answering the question directly.
  • RReason Why it is true: the logic, often with because, since or so.
  • EEvidence / Example Proof: a fact, a figure or a scene, ideally from Hong Kong.
  • IImpact So what? The consequence for the reader, and the link back to your stance.

Reports and stories use their own patterns; see Text-type features.

One paragraph, three levels

Practice task: should secondary schools start at 9 a.m. instead of 8 a.m.?

3 Claim and a vague example, then the claim again

CFirstly, schools should start later. EFor example, many students sleep very late because they have a lot of homework. CSo schools should start later.

No reason, no impact. The last sentence repeats the first, so the paragraph goes in a circle.

5 All four moves, in order

CFirst, a later start would improve students' health. RTeenagers' body clocks naturally run later, so asking them to be alert at 8 a.m. works against their biology. EMany S5 students in my school sleep after midnight because of homework and tutorial classes, yet leave home before 7. IAs a result, they arrive tired and learn less in their first lessons.

Complete and logical. The example is real but general, and the impact stops at "learn less".

5** All four moves, each one sharpened

CThe strongest case for a later start is not comfort but learning. RSleep research shows that a teenager's body clock runs up to two hours later than an adult's, so an 8 a.m. lesson asks students to think at the hour their brains are least ready. EWalk into any S5 classroom at 8:15 and the evidence is in front of you: heads on desks, a quiz half-finished, a teacher repeating instructions for the third time. IIf the first two lessons of every day are lost to fatigue, we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

The claim answers the obvious objection ("it's just about comfort"). The example is a scene you can see. The impact turns tiredness into a cost everyone cares about.

Practise: label each sentence

Each paragraph below is jumbled. Tap C, R, E or I for every sentence, then check. A wrong label gets a hint, not the answer.

Task: should phones be allowed in lessons?

  1. When my English teacher let us check collocations on our phones during a writing lesson, the whole class corrected mistakes we had been making all term.

  2. Schools should allow phones in lessons, but only as learning tools.

  3. Banning phones outright would throw away a tool that, used well, makes students more independent learners.

  4. Used with clear rules, a phone gives every student instant access to dictionaries, news and practice tools that a textbook cannot match.

Task: should the plastic levy cover takeaway containers?

  1. A small charge changes habits because people notice the cost every single time they pay it.

  2. Applying the same idea to containers could cut one of the largest sources of waste in our landfills.

  3. The government should extend the plastic levy to all takeaway containers.

  4. After Hong Kong's plastic bag levy began, carrying a reusable bag quickly became normal at the supermarket checkout.

Check your own paragraph

The last sentence, three levels

3So schools should start later.

5As a result, they arrive tired and learn less in their first lessons.

5**…we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

CLY: The Impact is where Level 5 becomes 5**: never end a paragraph by repeating its first line.

A whole letter, analysed

The plan from Step 0, written out as a letter to the editor (about 400 words). Read each paragraph, then the note under it.

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to your article "Should the school bell ring later?" (12 March). As an S5 student who leaves home at 6:45 every morning, I strongly support moving the start of the secondary school day to 9 a.m., and I believe the benefits far outweigh the costs.

Opening. Why I'm writing (the article and its date), why I'm worth listening to (an S5 student at 6:45), and my stance in one sentence. No "Firstly" yet: the reader knows where the letter is going.

CThe strongest case for a later start is not comfort but learning. RSleep research shows that a teenager's body clock runs up to two hours later than an adult's, so an 8 a.m. lesson asks students to think at the hour their brains are least ready. EWalk into any S5 classroom at 8:15 and the evidence is in front of you: heads on desks, a quiz half-finished, a teacher repeating instructions for the third time. IIf the first two lessons of every day are lost to fatigue, we are paying full price for an education students only half receive.

Body 1, CREI. The strongest argument goes first. "Not comfort but learning" answers the obvious objection before anyone raises it.

CA later start would also protect students' mental health. RSleep loss does not just make teenagers tired; it makes them more anxious and less able to cope with pressure. EMany of my classmates finish homework and tutorial worksheets after midnight, then sleep barely six hours before the next school day begins. IAn extra hour each morning will not remove exam pressure, but it would give students the rest they need to face it.

Body 2, CREI. "Also" links it to the first point. The Impact admits a limit ("will not remove exam pressure"), which makes the claim more believable, not less.

Admittedly, critics worry that a later start would clash with parents' working hours and push extracurricular activities into the evening. These concerns are real, but they are practical problems with practical solutions. Schools could open their libraries from 7:30 for students who must arrive early, and some activities could move to lunchtime or one free afternoon a week.

The other side. Concede ("Admittedly… These concerns are real"), then answer with solutions, not just disagreement. This paragraph is what most Level 4 letters are missing.

I therefore urge the Education Bureau to invite a small group of secondary schools to trial a 9 a.m. start for one year, and to publish the results on attendance, grades and well-being. If the evidence is as clear as I expect, Hong Kong students could start each day ready to learn, rather than simply present.

Closing. A recommendation that is specific (who, what, how long, how to measure it) and a last line that echoes the learning argument from Body 1.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

The closing, three levels

3In conclusion, I think schools should start later. Thank you.

5I hope the government will consider letting schools start at 9 a.m.

5**I therefore urge the Education Bureau to invite a small group of schools to trial a 9 a.m. start for one year…

CLY: A letter is organised when every paragraph has one job and the reader could name it.

Text-type features

Each text type has its own reader, register and frame. Get the frame right, then spend your energy on the content.

Article

An article is written for readers of a magazine, newsletter or website. It has to earn their attention from the headline onwards.

Reader
Magazine or newsletter readers
Register
Lively, but well organised
Format
Headline · byline · paragraphs
Paragraphs
CREI
  1. HeadlineShort and specific. A question, a pun or a bold claim.
  2. Hook paragraphA scene, a surprising fact or a question the reader can't ignore.
  3. Two or three CREI body paragraphsOne idea each, with an example from Hong Kong life.
  4. EndingReturn to your opening image, or leave the reader with something to do.
  • Give your article a headline. Many students forget, and it's part of the format.
  • Use subheadings only if the publication would. A website article, yes; a short magazine piece, rarely.
  • "In conclusion," at the end of an article. It sounds like an essay. End with an image or a call to action.

Phrases worth borrowing

  • Picture this: …
  • It's a familiar story: …
  • What most people don't realise is that …
  • The numbers tell only half the story.
  • So, what can we actually do?
  • Next time you …, remember …

Letter to the editor

You're writing to a newspaper to argue a point in public. The editor is your addressee, but your real audience is every reader of that newspaper.

Reader
The public, through the editor
Register
Formal and reasoned
Format
"Dear Editor," … "Yours faithfully,"
Paragraphs
CREI
  1. Why you're writingRefer to the article or issue and state your view in one sentence.
  2. Argument one (CREI)Your strongest point, with evidence or an example.
  3. Argument two (CREI)A different angle: cost, fairness, health, the environment.
  4. The other sideAdmit one opposing point, then answer it.
  5. What should happenA clear, realistic recommendation.
  • "Yours faithfully," then a name. The task may give you a pen name such as Chris Wong; use it.
  • Use formal linking: "Furthermore", "Admittedly", "It follows that".
  • Contractions (don't, it's) in a formal letter.

Speech

A speech is writing for the ear. If it doesn't sound good read aloud, it isn't finished.

Reader
A live audience
Register
Direct, warm, persuasive
Format
Greeting · body · thanks
Paragraphs
CREI, with signposts
  1. Greeting"Good morning, Principal, teachers and fellow students."
  2. Introduce yourself and the topicWho you are and why you're speaking today.
  3. Signpost"I'd like to share three reasons…" Listeners can't flip back a page.
  4. Body (CREI)One point at a time, with a story or a question to the audience.
  5. Call to action and thanksTell them exactly what to do, then "Thank you."
  • Use "you" and "we" often. Ask the audience questions.
  • Use the rule of three: three examples, three adjectives, three short sentences.
  • Writing a speech that reads like an essay with a greeting stuck on top.

Proposal

A proposal asks someone with power, such as a principal or a committee, to approve a plan. It wins by being practical, not passionate.

Reader
A decision-maker
Register
Formal and businesslike
Format
To · From · Date · Subject, then headings
Paragraphs
CREI under each heading
  1. HeaderTo: The Principal · From: Chris Wong, Student Union · Date · Subject: Proposal for a reading corner
  2. IntroductionThe purpose of the proposal, in one or two sentences.
  3. BackgroundThe problem now, with evidence: a survey, an observation.
  4. The planWhat, where, when, who, and what it costs.
  5. Benefits (CREI)Who gains and how.
  6. ConclusionA polite request for approval.
  • Use headings. A busy principal should be able to skim them.
  • Use "would" and "could" to sound reasonable: "This would allow…"
  • Emotional pleading: "Please, please approve this, students really need it!"

Report

A report tells a reader what was found and what should be done. Facts first, opinions last, and clearly labelled.

Reader
Someone who asked for it
Register
Formal, objective
Format
Title or memo header, headings
Paragraphs
Finding → evidence → recommendation
  1. Title or header"Report on Students' Use of the School Canteen"
  2. IntroductionWho asked for the report, its purpose, and how information was collected.
  3. FindingsOne heading per area: the finding, then the evidence (figures, comments). Past tense, mostly.
  4. RecommendationsWhat should change, each one linked to a finding.
  5. ConclusionA one-paragraph summary.
  • Use the passive where it sounds natural: "A survey was conducted…", "It was found that…"
  • Keep opinions in the Recommendations section.
  • Writing "I think" in the Findings section.

Story

A story is judged on how well you control the reader's experience. Show what happened; don't just report it.

Reader
Usually a competition or magazine
Register
Vivid, controlled
Format
Title, paragraphs, dialogue
Paragraphs
Scene → feeling → turn
  1. Start in the middleBegin with action or dialogue, not "One day…"
  2. Build tensionEach paragraph: a scene, the feeling it causes, and a turn that moves the story on.
  3. The turning pointThe moment the character changes or learns something.
  4. A short endingLink back to the question's prompt or the first line.
  • Keep your tenses consistent: tell the story in the past tense.
  • Punctuate dialogue correctly: "Ready?" she whispered.
  • Ending with "…and then I woke up. It was all a dream."
