Q4Letter to the editorYouth & society

Hong Kong now has many more areas such as parks, shops and restaurants that welcome dogs. Some people feel that the city has gone too far in its efforts to be dog-friendly.

The Hong Kong Times is asking readers for their opinion. Write a letter to the editor expressing whether or not you support this new trend.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

The task, reviewed

Hong Kong now has many more areas such as parks, shops and restaurants that welcome dogs. Some people feel that the city has gone too far in its efforts to be dog-friendly. The Hong Kong Times is asking readers for their opinion. Write a letter to the editor expressing whether or not you support this new trend.

The ideas

  • For: owners can bring dogs along instead of leaving them at home; dogs as company; a more open, modern city.
  • Against: many people are afraid of dogs; a crowded city has no room for them; restaurants and malls lack the facilities.
  • Use the question's words: "gone too far" invites a middle position: support the trend, but not everywhere.

From the official exemplars: the same idea at two levels

Both scripts argue against the trend, and both start from the same idea: some people are afraid of dogs.

3 "…some of the people living in Hong Kong are scared of dogs. If most of the areas in Hong Kong become dogs-friendly, it will definitately affort people's life."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3 (the candidate's spelling is kept)

5 "It is not a rare experience for me to encounter startled pedestrians as I walk my dogs on streets, indicating that a significant proportion of people are not comfortable with dogs."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3

What changed: the Level 3 writer states the idea and imagines a scene. The Level 5 writer is a dog owner arguing against dog-friendly places, so their own experience becomes evidence the reader trusts, and "indicating that" turns one walk into a conclusion. The Level 5 letter also ends on a line worth borrowing: "the city itself cannot be dog-friendly. Only we humans can."

One step further: the same idea at 5**

5** Experience, a local setting, and a sharper impact

Even as a dog owner, I have lost count of the pedestrians who step into the road when my two dogs come towards them on a narrow Mong Kok pavement. They are not unkind or unreasonable; they are frightened, and in a city where a pavement is often barely wide enough for two people, they have nowhere else to go. A trend that asks them to share restaurants and buses with dogs as well has not made the city friendlier. It has simply decided whose comfort counts.

It keeps the Level 5 writer's best move (a dog owner's own experience), adds a place the reader can picture, and ends with an impact that reframes the whole debate as a question of fairness.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear editor,

I am writing to share my point of view of the new trend, which is dog-friendly. There is no denying that dogs are human's friend. However, I do not support this new trend.

First and foremost, some of the people living in Hong Kong are scared of dogs. If most of the areas in Hong Kong become dogs-friendly, it will definitately affort people's life. For example, when you are eating burger in the fast food restaurant, a huge dog sites next to you, keeps making some terriable sounds and seems like it is going to attack you. Therefore, enhancing of dog-friendly place is not friendly to people who do not like dogs.

Furthermore, it is vital that the dogs should be under control at all times. Unfortunately, not all of the dogs owners can do it. When the owner do not keep their dog on a leash as well as under control all the times, it is not merely dangerous to the dogs but also other people. For instance, there is plenty of things that will kill dogs if they eat it, including chocolate, chicken bonds. Another example is the dogs may run away because the places which are dog-friendly are not mostly for the dogs, there may be some loud noise, or too crowded, that will scared them as well.

On top of that, there are lots of news mentioned that the causes of dogs attacking people are increasing. Thanks to the trend, dogs are allowed to go to more different places, such as parks, which must be full of children. Some children are too young and do not know how to play with the dogs. If they do something that will draw dogs' attention or let them become excited, like running. The dogs may attack them. When I was young, I play with a dog and start running, it keeps following me. I do not like it but the dog did not stop. Lastly, this continued for half hours long, which really scared me. Until now, I still scared of dogs. Therefore, dogs may attack people or leave some terrible memories to people.

In conclusion, above are the reasons why I do not support the new trend. I hope my point of view will be helpful. Thanks for your time.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3. Errors that block meaning are underlined.

Content
A clear stance and three reasons, and the childhood story is relevant. But the second reason drifts away from the question (chocolate and chicken bones are about dog safety, not the trend), and nothing here is specific to Hong Kong.
Organisation
The letter frame and linking words are all there ("First and foremost", "Furthermore", "On top of that"). Inside each paragraph, though, ideas pile up without a clear line, and the conclusion only says "above are the reasons".
Language
Frequent errors in verb forms and spelling ("affort", "sites", "I still scared") make the reader work to understand. The story slips between past and present.

To reach Level 4: check every verb, keep one idea per paragraph, and cut the example that has stopped answering the question.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear Editor,

Recently, the issue of whether more areas in Hong Kong should be set to be dog-friendly has aroused wide concern and provoked a stir among the locals. Advocates put forward the idea that it is more convenient for dog-owners to bring them for a walk, while the skeptics insist that it may negatively affect other pedestrians and the environment. Divergent as the arguments are, it is my firm conviction that the new trend should be supported.

Among the reasons for my stance, the first recurring to my mind is that having more dog-friendly areas is improving convenience. In the old days, when different parks, shops and restaurants inhibit the entrance of dogs, their owners had to bring them back to their home before entering these areas. They had to spend time and energy to do this which is not as meaningful as we may think. Inevitably, dogs had also lost myriad of opportunities to spend with their owners. According to research carried out by Hong Kong University, the major reason for people not to have a dog is because there were inadequate dog-friendly areas in the city. Having more of them is beneficial to dog-owners, and may attract more dog-lovers to own a dog.

Alongside improving convenience, under no circumstances should we overlook the fact that having more dog-friendly areas can allow people to have a companion with them while outside home. Sometimes, your friends may not be vacant to go shopping with you or go dining in the new restaurant near the promenade together. Undoubtedly, this is quite upset and lonely for us. When more areas are open to dogs, we can simply bring along our dog with us, which is publicly-recognized as our "loyal friend". In Hong Kong, numerous dog-owners are seen everyday, no matter they are shopping in the mall or having a walk in the park.

Last but not least, it is of paramount importance that we notice the fact that having more dog-friendly areas is helping us to build a fair society. Dogs are living-organisms, as well as our pets. They should have their rights and freedom under this modern and advanced world. We always emphasize that discrimination should be eliminated, but actually this also applies on dogs. Opening more areas to dogs plays an important role to achieve a fair society.

Some people may say that dogs will bark on other people and excrete their "waste" anywhere on the street. However, it is a fact that most Hong Kong citizens are obeying the rules. They keep their dog on a leash and so the dog is under control at all times. After the dog goes to the toilet, their owners clean up the waste products immediately, maintaining the public hygiene.

To conclude, it is my firm belief that the trend of having more areas that welcome dogs should be supported. This is advantageous to both humans and dogs in different aspects. It is earnestly hoped that more areas will open to dogs and more policies will be released by the government to help the dogs to blend with the city.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 4 exemplar 3. Errors are underlined.

Content
Three different reasons (convenience, company, fairness) and a separate answer to the other side. The support is thin: the "research by Hong Kong University" is vague, and the fairness point is asserted, not argued.
Organisation
A strong opening that sets out both sides before giving a stance, one reason per paragraph, and a rebuttal paragraph. The conclusion is general ("advantageous… in different aspects").
Language
Ambitious, mostly accurate structures ("Divergent as the arguments are", "under no circumstances should we overlook"), but many phrases are formulaic, and word-choice errors remain ("vacant", "applies on").

What moved it up from Level 3: accurate complex sentences, a balanced opening and a real rebuttal. To reach Level 5: replace general claims with Hong Kong evidence, and develop each reason instead of stating it.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear Editor,

I am writing to share my two cents on whether or not Hong Kong should be more dog-friendly. Many have opined that welcoming pet dogs in restaurants, shops and even buses is a major leap towards building a dog-friendly city. However, I believe that our city is incompatible with such trends and relaxing dog restrictions is merely a gesture that yields no benefit to the city, humans and even the pet dogs themselves.

There is a reason why estates in Hong Kong typically prohibit keeping dogs as pets. Dogs are not to everyone's liking and can easily frighten or disturb others. It is obvious that some people are frightened by dogs, while some others are simply unfamiliar with dogs and feel uncomfortable when dogs are in close proximity. Even though I am a dog owner, I still find it totally reasonable that some people may have different — and even opposite — sentiments towards dogs than mine. It is not a rare experience for me to encounter startled pedestrians as I walk my dogs on streets, indicating that a significant proportion of people are not comfortable with dogs. Transforming Hong Kong into a dog-friendly city is therefore unwise as it would mean that people will encounter dogs more frequently and the likelihood of someone feeling uncomfortable rises.

Coupled with the crowded nature of our city, the negative effects of welcoming dogs only compounds in magnitude. Many proponents of the trend cite examples of the already-pet-friendly in the Western Hemisphere. Yet Hong Kong is one of the most densely populated city in the world, a city of stark contrast with many Western cities with numerous open spaces. Not to mention that the proportion of pet owners is significantly lower locally. In Hong Kong, many shops only have narrow aisles for people to pass through and crowded trains and buses epitomize the lack of space of our city. If pet dogs are allowed into these crowded places, those who are not comfortable with dogs will only feel even worse. Even those who are comfortable with dogs will find it inconvenient as dogs often have different paces to humans when it comes to walking. As a dog owner, I would also be concerned that my dogs will be injured easily in crowded areas as dogs can be difficult to spot in a crowd and a foot may land on their delicate paws.

Furthermore, our crowded metropolis also has insufficient facilities to cater to the needs of dogs. If our city were to transform into a truly dog-friendly city, the coverage and number of dog facilities must scale up by a great magnitude. The majority of restaurants do not actually have enough space for even one dog to stay, let alone a few dogs. The restaurant owners are not to blame for that, but it is simply an irrefutable fact that our city's developments were not designed to accommodate dogs in public places on a large scale. Other facilities such as litter bins for dogs are also rarely seen outside of parks. Litter bins need to be placed in malls for dog owners to conveniently dispose dogs' wastes if dogs were to be allowed in such spaces.

'Dog-friendly' is not a buzzword, nor is welcoming dogs merely a friendly gesture. Being 'dog-friendly' goes far beyond removing restrictions of where dogs can go. At the crux of a dog-friendly city is the harmonious co-existence of humans and dogs without causing harm to their quality of life. There needs to be more planning before our city becomes truly dog-friendly. More importantly, Hong Kong people have to reach a consensus regarding the extent dogs should be allowed in the neighbourhood. Because ultimately, the city itself cannot be dog-friendly. Only we humans can.

Yours truly,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3.

Content
An unexpected angle (a dog owner arguing against the trend) that makes every point more believable. The reasons belong to Hong Kong: housing estates that ban dogs, narrow shop aisles, crowded trains. It answers the other side inside the argument ("Many proponents… cite examples… Yet Hong Kong is…") and ends with a balanced, thoughtful position.
Organisation
Each paragraph has one job and a clear topic sentence, and the links carry the logic forward ("Coupled with…", "Furthermore…"). The closing paragraph does not repeat the reasons; it redefines what "dog-friendly" should mean, and ends on a line the reader remembers.
Language
Precise, varied vocabulary used naturally ("epitomize", "irrefutable", "cater to the needs of"), dashes for emphasis, and a confident rhythm. A few slips remain: "the negative effects… only compounds", "one of the most densely populated city", and "Yours truly" after "Dear Editor".

What moved it up from Level 4: Hong Kong evidence instead of general claims, first-hand experience used as proof, the other side answered inside the argument, and a conclusion that adds a new idea.

The Level 5 letter already has a 5** idea. What holds it back is length and a few loose sentences. Here is its opening and closing, rewritten by CLY.

CLY's rewrite · not an exam script

Dear Editor,

As a dog owner, I might be expected to welcome every new "dog-friendly" sign in Hong Kong. I do not. A city where pavements are barely wide enough for two people, and where most housing estates still ban pets, is not ready to share its restaurants and buses with dogs, and pretending that it is helps neither residents nor the dogs themselves.

[…three body paragraphs, each cut by about a third…]

"Dog-friendly" is not a buzzword, nor is it a list of places where dogs are allowed. It is a promise that people and dogs can share a city without either side paying for it. Until we have the space, the facilities and, above all, an agreement on where dogs belong, that promise is premature. A city cannot be dog-friendly on its own. Only its people can.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

What changed
The opening turns the writer's identity into a hook ("I might be expected to… I do not."). Every sentence is shorter and does one job. The closing keeps the Level 5 writer's best line, and the sign-off is now correct for "Dear Editor".
