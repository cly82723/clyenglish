Where these questions come from

These are CLY's summaries of each question, not the wording of the exam paper. The papers belong to the HKEAA, which sells them through its publications page. Always practise from the real paper when you can.

Each year's summaries were written from the sources named under that year. A blue text type links to CLY's guide for it. Up to 2023, each Part B question belonged to one of eight elective modules, shown beside the question; from 2024 there are four Part B questions and no electives.

The sample answers in the 2024 and 2025 cards quote real candidates from the HKEAA's official exemplars, marked by examiners (from the HKEAA website). They are typed from the candidates' handwriting with their mistakes kept, because the mistakes are part of the level. The official exemplars stop at Level 5, so each 5** version is CLY's own. The older cards quote tutors' published samples, so treat their levels as the tutors' claims. The analysis is CLY's.

Q2Story

A friend went through an event that changed him greatly. Write a short story about it.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

Your friend experienced an event that significantly changed him. Write a short story about this.

All three candidates set their story at a theme park and meet an old schoolmate who has changed, so you can compare them directly. The stories are long, so key passages are shown, with the rest summarised in square brackets.

The story at three levels, and a rewrite

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Recently, I am having work at a theme park, Ocean Park. Today, I back to Ocean Park to work as normal. When I was having my duty, I saw an old classmate, Chris Wong, who behaved badly when I was at school in Ocean Park too. I was surprised and wondering why he is a little bit different, he seems like softer and friendly than before, is it my illusion? In my memory, Chris Wong always mad and love fighting with others. Then I told him my thought, and he told me about an important event that happened last year and this made him a better person.

[Chris left school, was fired from a restaurant for being rude, attacked his manager and was sent to jail. A social worker found that he had copied his violent father, and helped him.]

…The social worker helped him to pass through the mental illness slowly. She healed his heart. After a month, Chris recovered and he became a better person.

This is the story that why Chris become a better person. And Chris told me, now he will not fight with others anymore and he also can control his emotion. He said, "I am very thankful. Without the social worker, I can't be the better me."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 1. Errors are underlined.

Content
A complete story of change with a clear cause (the social worker). But almost everything is summarised, not shown, and the change is simply stated: "he became a better person" appears three times.
Organisation
Clear and chronological. The theme-park meeting opens the story but is never returned to, so the ending is a summary ("This is the story that…") instead of a final scene.
Language
Tense errors throughout ("I am having work", "I back to"), simple and repeated vocabulary, and almost no dialogue until the last line.

To reach Level 4: turn two of the summarised events into short scenes with dialogue, and fix the tenses: a story is told in the past.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

CONSEQUENCES & PENITENCE

Halfway through the day, I found myself bored wandering about the theme park. I muttered to myself "why did I go here again?" as I remember, reaching through my pockets I see a crumpled up ticket from the letter coming from my mom. She was worried that I have stayed in my room the past week without any hint of sunlight…

By sheer coincidence, at the corner of my eye, I see a familiar face that I wish I could forget. It was this high-school bully that everyone hated… I put on a façade of politeness as I greet him: "Hey! How have you been!" "Wassup my man! Let me get you a drink at that restaurant over there."

…Taking a second look at him, he seems more polite and soft, although his tough exterior is still apparent. "Stuff happens, you know?" his tone lowered as a sombre expression washes over his face.

[He tells how an accident left his mother in a coma, how he worked to support her with no one to help, and how he has been asking everyone he hurt to forgive him.]

I guess karma does exist, the golden rule of "you reap what you sow" is actually true and kindness is met with kindness, and of course vice versa.

~ THE END ~

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 4 exemplar 1.

Content
A real character with a backstory, and a change that is partly shown ("more polite and soft, although his tough exterior is still apparent"). The ending falls back on a moral cliché ("you reap what you sow") instead of a moment.
Organisation
A strong set-up (the crumpled ticket, the unwanted meeting) and a flashback told through dialogue. The second half rushes, and the opening's ticket returns only briefly.
Language
Lively, idiomatic phrases ("put on a façade of politeness", "a sombre expression washes over his face"), but the tense jumps between present and past almost every sentence.

What moved it up from Level 3: scenes and dialogue instead of summary, and vivid phrases. To reach Level 5: keep one tense, and end on a scene instead of a moral.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Life On A Ride

"Brother, brother," my younger sister, Mary, pulled on my sleeve, "I want to ride that."

I glanced over to the ride she was pointing towards. A neon sign over the entrance says 'Rusty Rumble' in bold letters. I looked down and saw a sign that says 'Expected queue time: 60 minutes'. Suddenly, the neon sign did not look appealing at all…

"Chris?" as I arrived to the queue, I heard a familiar voice call out to me. I looked up and gasped in shock. He certainly looked… different, to say the least. Back in the days when we were classmates, he dyed his fiery red hair and had piercings all over his body. His breath used to reek of the stench of cigarettes. Now, he's back to his natural hair colour, and there's not a single piercing in sight.

Peter chuckled, "Yeah, life's been a heck of a ride."

[In the queue, Peter tells how anxiety and depression, and parents who would not listen, led to a hospital stay; how the friends he made there changed how he saw things; and how his family was waiting for him when he came out. He is now repeating Form 6.]

He returned the hug and didn't say anything, but I could feel him smiling. His warmth no longer came with the stench of cigarettes, but radiated courage and determination to change and become a better person.

"Next!" the ride operator shouted towards the queue. Mary, Peter, and I moved onto the ride. As the seatbelts fastened, Mary squeezed my hand…

"Don't be scared," I comforted her, "For now, it's only going up!"

"Yeah, only going up," Peter said. "Even if it falls, you'll be back here safe and sound in no time."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 1. The middle section deals with mental illness and is summarised.

Content
The change is shown through details you can see and smell (red hair, piercings, cigarettes → natural hair, no piercings), and the serious theme is handled with care. The ride becomes a metaphor for Peter's life, signalled in the title and in "life's been a heck of a ride".
Organisation
A frame story: it opens in the queue, Peter's story happens while they wait, and it closes on the ride itself. The last lines ("only going up… even if it falls") echo his whole story.
Language
Natural dialogue that moves the story, vivid sensory detail, and controlled punctuation. Small slips remain ("I arrived to the queue", tense shifts in the signs).

What moved it up from Level 4: a frame and a metaphor that hold the story together, change shown through detail, and an ending that is a scene, not a moral.

The Level 5 story has a 5** structure. What would lift it is tighter sentences and a final image that lands. Here are its reveal and ending, rewritten by CLY.

CLY's rewrite · not an exam script

"Chris?" The voice came from the queue behind us, and it took me a moment to place it. The last time I had seen Peter, his hair was fire-engine red, there were rings in both eyebrows, and he smelt of the cigarettes he smoked behind the school hall. The boy in front of me had black hair, a plain grey hoodie and nothing in his hands but a ride ticket. Only his grin was the same.

[…Peter's story, told in the queue…]

"Next!" the operator called. As the bar clicked down across our laps, Mary grabbed my hand. "It's only going up for now," I told her. Beside us, Peter laughed and loosened his grip on the rail. "And when it drops," he said, "you still end up back where you started, only a little braver."

What changed
The reveal is one tight paragraph of contrasts, ending on the one thing that has not changed. The final image (Peter letting go of the rail) shows his change in an action, and his last line finishes the ride metaphor.

Q4Essay

Discuss the challenges of group work in the workplace and suggest how it could be improved.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

Discuss the challenges of group work in the workplace and provide suggestions for improvement. Write an essay about this.

The ideas

  • Challenges: clashing schedules and working styles; slow decisions and endless meetings; nobody feeling responsible; conflict between members.
  • Suggestions: one named owner per task; clear deadlines; shared online tools; short regular check-ins.
  • Pair them: every suggestion should solve a challenge you have already described. A list of tips that answers nothing loses Content marks.

From the official exemplars: the same idea at two levels

Both scripts open with the same challenge: team members work at different times.

3 "One of the major challenges of group work in the workplace is that employees have different working schedule."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3 (the candidate's grammar is kept)

5 "For example, a member who prefers working at night would find it difficult to agree on a meeting time with another member who likes working during the day."

5 "When tasked as a group, individuals tend to feel less responsible for the task, as they can easily push the responsibility onto another member."

HKEAA 2025 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3

What changed: the Level 3 writer names the challenge. The Level 5 writer shows it with two specific people, then finds a deeper challenge most candidates miss: when a task belongs to everyone, nobody feels responsible for it.

From a published 5** sample

"When one individual falls behind, the entire group bears the consequences."

Sample on PL Learning

What works: one short sentence carries a whole cause and effect, which makes a strong topic sentence. The same sample pairs each challenge with a practical fix, such as named roles, fixed deadlines and shared planning tools.

One step further: the same idea at 5**

5** The Level 5 idea, named, shown and solved

The quietest problem in any team is the one no one owns. When a task belongs to five people, each can assume that someone else will chase the supplier or check the figures, and on the morning of the deadline the team discovers that everyone assumed. Psychologists call this diffusion of responsibility; managers simply call it a missed deadline. Naming one owner for every task, however small, costs nothing and removes the place where work goes to hide.

A memorable claim, a scene from a real office, the proper name for the effect, and a suggestion that answers exactly this challenge.

Q4Letter to the editor

Hong Kong now has many dog-friendly parks, shops and restaurants, and some people think this has gone too far. Tell the Hong Kong Times whether you support the trend.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

The task, reviewed

Hong Kong now has many more areas such as parks, shops and restaurants that welcome dogs. Some people feel that the city has gone too far in its efforts to be dog-friendly. The Hong Kong Times is asking readers for their opinion. Write a letter to the editor expressing whether or not you support this new trend.

The ideas

  • For: owners can bring dogs along instead of leaving them at home; dogs as company; a more open, modern city.
  • Against: many people are afraid of dogs; a crowded city has no room for them; restaurants and malls lack the facilities.
  • Use the question's words: "gone too far" invites a middle position: support the trend, but not everywhere.

From the official exemplars: the same idea at two levels

Both scripts argue against the trend, and both start from the same idea: some people are afraid of dogs.

3 "…some of the people living in Hong Kong are scared of dogs. If most of the areas in Hong Kong become dogs-friendly, it will definitately affort people's life."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3 (the candidate's spelling is kept)

5 "It is not a rare experience for me to encounter startled pedestrians as I walk my dogs on streets, indicating that a significant proportion of people are not comfortable with dogs."

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3

What changed: the Level 3 writer states the idea and imagines a scene. The Level 5 writer is a dog owner arguing against dog-friendly places, so their own experience becomes evidence the reader trusts, and "indicating that" turns one walk into a conclusion. The Level 5 letter also ends on a line worth borrowing: "the city itself cannot be dog-friendly. Only we humans can."

One step further: the same idea at 5**

5** Experience, a local setting, and a sharper impact

Even as a dog owner, I have lost count of the pedestrians who step into the road when my two dogs come towards them on a narrow Mong Kok pavement. They are not unkind or unreasonable; they are frightened, and in a city where a pavement is often barely wide enough for two people, they have nowhere else to go. A trend that asks them to share restaurants and buses with dogs as well has not made the city friendlier. It has simply decided whose comfort counts.

It keeps the Level 5 writer's best move (a dog owner's own experience), adds a place the reader can picture, and ends with an impact that reframes the whole debate as a question of fairness.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear editor,

I am writing to share my point of view of the new trend, which is dog-friendly. There is no denying that dogs are human's friend. However, I do not support this new trend.

First and foremost, some of the people living in Hong Kong are scared of dogs. If most of the areas in Hong Kong become dogs-friendly, it will definitately affort people's life. For example, when you are eating burger in the fast food restaurant, a huge dog sites next to you, keeps making some terriable sounds and seems like it is going to attack you. Therefore, enhancing of dog-friendly place is not friendly to people who do not like dogs.

Furthermore, it is vital that the dogs should be under control at all times. Unfortunately, not all of the dogs owners can do it. When the owner do not keep their dog on a leash as well as under control all the times, it is not merely dangerous to the dogs but also other people. For instance, there is plenty of things that will kill dogs if they eat it, including chocolate, chicken bonds. Another example is the dogs may run away because the places which are dog-friendly are not mostly for the dogs, there may be some loud noise, or too crowded, that will scared them as well.

On top of that, there are lots of news mentioned that the causes of dogs attacking people are increasing. Thanks to the trend, dogs are allowed to go to more different places, such as parks, which must be full of children. Some children are too young and do not know how to play with the dogs. If they do something that will draw dogs' attention or let them become excited, like running. The dogs may attack them. When I was young, I play with a dog and start running, it keeps following me. I do not like it but the dog did not stop. Lastly, this continued for half hours long, which really scared me. Until now, I still scared of dogs. Therefore, dogs may attack people or leave some terrible memories to people.

In conclusion, above are the reasons why I do not support the new trend. I hope my point of view will be helpful. Thanks for your time.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 3 exemplar 3. Errors that block meaning are underlined.

Content
A clear stance and three reasons, and the childhood story is relevant. But the second reason drifts away from the question (chocolate and chicken bones are about dog safety, not the trend), and nothing here is specific to Hong Kong.
Organisation
The letter frame and linking words are all there ("First and foremost", "Furthermore", "On top of that"). Inside each paragraph, though, ideas pile up without a clear line, and the conclusion only says "above are the reasons".
Language
Frequent errors in verb forms and spelling ("affort", "sites", "I still scared") make the reader work to understand. The story slips between past and present.

To reach Level 4: check every verb, keep one idea per paragraph, and cut the example that has stopped answering the question.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear Editor,

Recently, the issue of whether more areas in Hong Kong should be set to be dog-friendly has aroused wide concern and provoked a stir among the locals. Advocates put forward the idea that it is more convenient for dog-owners to bring them for a walk, while the skeptics insist that it may negatively affect other pedestrians and the environment. Divergent as the arguments are, it is my firm conviction that the new trend should be supported.

Among the reasons for my stance, the first recurring to my mind is that having more dog-friendly areas is improving convenience. In the old days, when different parks, shops and restaurants inhibit the entrance of dogs, their owners had to bring them back to their home before entering these areas. They had to spend time and energy to do this which is not as meaningful as we may think. Inevitably, dogs had also lost myriad of opportunities to spend with their owners. According to research carried out by Hong Kong University, the major reason for people not to have a dog is because there were inadequate dog-friendly areas in the city. Having more of them is beneficial to dog-owners, and may attract more dog-lovers to own a dog.

Alongside improving convenience, under no circumstances should we overlook the fact that having more dog-friendly areas can allow people to have a companion with them while outside home. Sometimes, your friends may not be vacant to go shopping with you or go dining in the new restaurant near the promenade together. Undoubtedly, this is quite upset and lonely for us. When more areas are open to dogs, we can simply bring along our dog with us, which is publicly-recognized as our "loyal friend". In Hong Kong, numerous dog-owners are seen everyday, no matter they are shopping in the mall or having a walk in the park.

Last but not least, it is of paramount importance that we notice the fact that having more dog-friendly areas is helping us to build a fair society. Dogs are living-organisms, as well as our pets. They should have their rights and freedom under this modern and advanced world. We always emphasize that discrimination should be eliminated, but actually this also applies on dogs. Opening more areas to dogs plays an important role to achieve a fair society.

Some people may say that dogs will bark on other people and excrete their "waste" anywhere on the street. However, it is a fact that most Hong Kong citizens are obeying the rules. They keep their dog on a leash and so the dog is under control at all times. After the dog goes to the toilet, their owners clean up the waste products immediately, maintaining the public hygiene.

To conclude, it is my firm belief that the trend of having more areas that welcome dogs should be supported. This is advantageous to both humans and dogs in different aspects. It is earnestly hoped that more areas will open to dogs and more policies will be released by the government to help the dogs to blend with the city.

Yours faithfully,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 4 exemplar 3. Errors are underlined.

Content
Three different reasons (convenience, company, fairness) and a separate answer to the other side. The support is thin: the "research by Hong Kong University" is vague, and the fairness point is asserted, not argued.
Organisation
A strong opening that sets out both sides before giving a stance, one reason per paragraph, and a rebuttal paragraph. The conclusion is general ("advantageous… in different aspects").
Language
Ambitious, mostly accurate structures ("Divergent as the arguments are", "under no circumstances should we overlook"), but many phrases are formulaic, and word-choice errors remain ("vacant", "applies on").

What moved it up from Level 3: accurate complex sentences, a balanced opening and a real rebuttal. To reach Level 5: replace general claims with Hong Kong evidence, and develop each reason instead of stating it.

Real exam script · HKEAA exemplar

Dear Editor,

I am writing to share my two cents on whether or not Hong Kong should be more dog-friendly. Many have opined that welcoming pet dogs in restaurants, shops and even buses is a major leap towards building a dog-friendly city. However, I believe that our city is incompatible with such trends and relaxing dog restrictions is merely a gesture that yields no benefit to the city, humans and even the pet dogs themselves.

There is a reason why estates in Hong Kong typically prohibit keeping dogs as pets. Dogs are not to everyone's liking and can easily frighten or disturb others. It is obvious that some people are frightened by dogs, while some others are simply unfamiliar with dogs and feel uncomfortable when dogs are in close proximity. Even though I am a dog owner, I still find it totally reasonable that some people may have different — and even opposite — sentiments towards dogs than mine. It is not a rare experience for me to encounter startled pedestrians as I walk my dogs on streets, indicating that a significant proportion of people are not comfortable with dogs. Transforming Hong Kong into a dog-friendly city is therefore unwise as it would mean that people will encounter dogs more frequently and the likelihood of someone feeling uncomfortable rises.

Coupled with the crowded nature of our city, the negative effects of welcoming dogs only compounds in magnitude. Many proponents of the trend cite examples of the already-pet-friendly in the Western Hemisphere. Yet Hong Kong is one of the most densely populated city in the world, a city of stark contrast with many Western cities with numerous open spaces. Not to mention that the proportion of pet owners is significantly lower locally. In Hong Kong, many shops only have narrow aisles for people to pass through and crowded trains and buses epitomize the lack of space of our city. If pet dogs are allowed into these crowded places, those who are not comfortable with dogs will only feel even worse. Even those who are comfortable with dogs will find it inconvenient as dogs often have different paces to humans when it comes to walking. As a dog owner, I would also be concerned that my dogs will be injured easily in crowded areas as dogs can be difficult to spot in a crowd and a foot may land on their delicate paws.

Furthermore, our crowded metropolis also has insufficient facilities to cater to the needs of dogs. If our city were to transform into a truly dog-friendly city, the coverage and number of dog facilities must scale up by a great magnitude. The majority of restaurants do not actually have enough space for even one dog to stay, let alone a few dogs. The restaurant owners are not to blame for that, but it is simply an irrefutable fact that our city's developments were not designed to accommodate dogs in public places on a large scale. Other facilities such as litter bins for dogs are also rarely seen outside of parks. Litter bins need to be placed in malls for dog owners to conveniently dispose dogs' wastes if dogs were to be allowed in such spaces.

'Dog-friendly' is not a buzzword, nor is welcoming dogs merely a friendly gesture. Being 'dog-friendly' goes far beyond removing restrictions of where dogs can go. At the crux of a dog-friendly city is the harmonious co-existence of humans and dogs without causing harm to their quality of life. There needs to be more planning before our city becomes truly dog-friendly. More importantly, Hong Kong people have to reach a consensus regarding the extent dogs should be allowed in the neighbourhood. Because ultimately, the city itself cannot be dog-friendly. Only we humans can.

Yours truly,
Chris Wong

HKEAA 2024 exemplars, Level 5 exemplar 3.

Content
An unexpected angle (a dog owner arguing against the trend) that makes every point more believable. The reasons belong to Hong Kong: housing estates that ban dogs, narrow shop aisles, crowded trains. It answers the other side inside the argument ("Many proponents… cite examples… Yet Hong Kong is…") and ends with a balanced, thoughtful position.
Organisation
Each paragraph has one job and a clear topic sentence, and the links carry the logic forward ("Coupled with…", "Furthermore…"). The closing paragraph does not repeat the reasons; it redefines what "dog-friendly" should mean, and ends on a line the reader remembers.
Language
Precise, varied vocabulary used naturally ("epitomize", "irrefutable", "cater to the needs of"), dashes for emphasis, and a confident rhythm. A few slips remain: "the negative effects… only compounds", "one of the most densely populated city", and "Yours truly" after "Dear Editor".

What moved it up from Level 4: Hong Kong evidence instead of general claims, first-hand experience used as proof, the other side answered inside the argument, and a conclusion that adds a new idea.

The Level 5 letter already has a 5** idea. What holds it back is length and a few loose sentences. Here is its opening and closing, rewritten by CLY.

CLY's rewrite · not an exam script

Dear Editor,

As a dog owner, I might be expected to welcome every new "dog-friendly" sign in Hong Kong. I do not. A city where pavements are barely wide enough for two people, and where most housing estates still ban pets, is not ready to share its restaurants and buses with dogs, and pretending that it is helps neither residents nor the dogs themselves.

[…three body paragraphs, each cut by about a third…]

"Dog-friendly" is not a buzzword, nor is it a list of places where dogs are allowed. It is a promise that people and dogs can share a city without either side paying for it. Until we have the space, the facilities and, above all, an agreement on where dogs belong, that promise is premature. A city cannot be dog-friendly on its own. Only its people can.

Chris Wong

CLY's ideas and sample answers

A competition called "Stories of Survival" asks for a story on the theme "Nature: friend or enemy?". Write a story about a hiker caught in a storm in the mountains.

The ideas

  • Answer the theme: decide early whether nature turns out to be friend, enemy or both, and build towards that turn.
  • Use a real place: Lion Rock, Tai Mo Shan or the MacLehose Trail gives you details for free.
  • Five paragraphs of weather. The storm is the setting; the story is what the hiker decides.

From a published sample

The sample opens on a hiker named Emily in a mountain valley as the weather turns, then a sudden downpour turns the trail to mud.

Summary of the sample on Icecream Tutor

What works: it gets the hiker into the storm quickly. What to add: "the weather turns" is how most scripts begin. A 5** opening shows the storm through one small, specific detail.

One paragraph, three levels: the storm arrives

3 Telling the feeling

Suddenly, it rained very heavily. I was very scared. I did not know what to do.

"Suddenly", "very" twice, and the feeling is named instead of shown.

5 Showing the danger

Within minutes the drizzle became a downpour. The path turned to mud under my boots, and the mist swallowed the trail markers one by one. I realised I had no idea which way led down.

Scene and feeling are shown through what the hiker sees. The turn ("no idea which way") raises the stakes.

5** Scene, feeling, turn, and the theme

The first drop hit the map in my hand, and by the time I had folded it, the ridge behind me had disappeared. Rain drummed on my hood so loudly that I could no longer hear my own breathing. I counted the orange ribbons tied to the trees: three, then two, then none. The mountain had not changed; it had simply stopped showing me the way.

Small details carry the fear (the map, the ribbons counting down), and the last line answers the theme: nature is not cruel here, just indifferent.

Q5Debate speechDebating

At an inter-school debating competition, the motion is that the policy of reporting students' class position should be abolished. Write a speech for or against.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

You are representing your school at an inter-school debating competition. The motion is: "The policy of reporting students' class position should be abolished." Write a debate speech arguing for or against the motion.

The ideas

  • For: rankings damage motivation; they label students; they push learning towards marks instead of skills.
  • Against: rankings prepare students for competition; they give parents clear information; they reward effort.
  • Either side: answer the other team's strongest point, as the published sample does in its first paragraph.

From a published sample

"Examination is only a means to test our knowledge and learn from mistakes."

"This division has a labelling effect that stigmatizes students by putting them into categories."

Wendy Chan, 2018 DSE Writing Question 5 (sample)

What works: the first line goes back to what exams are for, which makes ranking look like a misuse of them. The second names a precise effect, labelling, instead of saying rankings are "unfair". What to add: a speech should sound spoken: address the judges, use a short scene, end lines with rhythm.

One paragraph, three levels: motivation

3 A feeling, not an argument

Firstly, class position is bad for students. It makes them sad and they do not want to study.

It could be true, but nothing explains how or proves it.

5 The mechanism, explained

Firstly, class position reporting damages motivation. When a report card says "35th out of 36", a student stops seeing exams as a way to learn and starts seeing them as proof that they have failed.

A concrete detail ("35th out of 36") and a clear chain of cause and effect. It still reads like an essay.

5** The same argument, written for the ear

Honourable judges, ask yourselves what "35th out of 36" teaches a fifteen-year-old. Not which topics to revise, not which mistakes to fix: only that thirty-four classmates did better. An exam should tell a student where to go next; a ranking only tells them where to stand. Abolish the ranking, and we give the exam back its purpose.

It speaks to the judges, uses a pattern you can hear ("not… not… only"), and ends on a balanced line built on the sample's best idea.

Q7ReportSocial Issues

For a project on Hong Kong's NEETs (young people not in education, employment or training), write a report explaining why their number is rising and suggesting how to help them.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

You are working on a project called "Hong Kong's NEETs": young people not in education, employment or training, many of whom spend their time at home playing video games or surfing the internet. Write a report explaining why the number of NEETs is rising and suggesting what can be done to help them, with reasons.

The ideas

  • Causes: overprotective parenting; neglect and lack of support in poorer families; a hard first step into work.
  • Help: social workers and support groups; subsidised part-time jobs as a first step; funding for further study.
  • Match them: each recommendation should answer one cause.

From a published sample

"…they fail to develop self-care ability and problem-solving skill and become overly dependent on their parents."

"Starting with a small step by taking a part-time job, they may feel easier to get on with work"

Wendy Chan, 2017 DSE Writing Question 7 (sample)

What works: the sample finds causes at both extremes, rich families and poor ones, which is a mature, balanced idea. Its recommendations are small, realistic steps. What to add: a report needs evidence under each finding (figures, interviews), and recommendations clearly linked back to findings.

One paragraph, three levels: a finding

3 A list of opinions

There are many reasons why NEETs are increasing. One reason is parents. Some parents spoil their children.

"Many reasons" and "spoil" are vague, and a report needs findings, not opinions.

5 A cause, explained

One cause is overprotective parenting. In some well-off families, parents handle every problem for their children, so the young people never learn to cope with difficulties on their own. When they face pressure at school or work, they simply withdraw.

A clear chain from cause to result, in a report's neutral voice, but it still needs evidence.

5** Finding, evidence, and the link to a recommendation

Finding 1: overprotection, not laziness. Social workers interviewed for this project described the same pattern: young people whose parents had chosen their schools, packed their bags and settled their arguments for them. Having never faced a problem alone, many found their first setback at work too much to bear, and retreated to the one place where nothing was expected of them. This suggests that support should build independence, not simply find jobs.

A heading that corrects a common assumption, evidence from the project, and a last line that leads straight into the recommendations. (The interviews are invented for practice; in the exam, invent sensible ones.)

Q7ProposalPopular Culture

The Hong Kong Youth Association is planning a Youth Festival to show trends popular with young people. Propose one or two trends, how to present them and why.

CLY's ideas and sample answers

The Hong Kong Youth Association is organising a Youth Festival to showcase popular youth trends in Hong Kong. Write a proposal suggesting one or two trends popular with young people, how they could be presented at the Festival, and why.

The ideas

  • Pick trends you can show, not just describe: street art, sustainable fashion, e-sports, cafés and latte art, hiking.
  • For each: what, how, why. What the trend is, how visitors experience it at the Festival, and why it deserves the space.
  • Describing the trend for 300 words and leaving "how to present it" to the last line.

From a published sample

"To showcase this trend at the festival, we propose setting up a dedicated 'Art Alley'."

Sample on Icecream Tutor

What works: "propose" plus a named, concrete plan. A proposal is judged on whether the reader can picture it happening. What to add: answer the practical questions (space, cost, permission) before the reader asks them.

One paragraph, three levels: presenting street art

3 A wish

We suggest street art. Many young people like it. We can let them draw on the wall.

Which wall? Who draws? Whose permission? A decision-maker can't approve this.

5 A plan with a reason

We propose an "Art Alley" where local street artists paint murals live during the Festival. Visitors could watch, ask questions and add their own designs to a community wall, so they take part in the trend instead of just looking at it.

What, who and why are answered. The practical problems are still open.

5** A plan that answers the objections

We propose turning the Festival's entrance corridor into an "Art Alley": six plywood panels, each painted live by a local street artist over the weekend, with the final panel left blank for visitors. Painting on removable boards avoids the obvious problem, permission to paint public walls, and the finished panels could tour community centres afterwards, so the trend outlives the Festival.

Where, how many, who, the objection solved, and a benefit that lasts. Every sentence makes approval easier.
