The language toolkit

Openings that make a marker look up

The marker has read many scripts before yours. Your first sentence decides whether they read yours with interest.

  • Nowadays, … In recent years, … As we all know, … With the development of technology, …

Five openings that work

  1. A scene"The MTR doors slide open at Mong Kok, and two hundred people check their phones at once."
  2. A question"When did you last spend an hour without a screen?"
  3. A surprising fact"Hong Kong throws away more food every day than…" (in the exam, a sensible estimate is fine; don't claim a precise source)
  4. A short, bold claim"Homework is not the enemy. Bad homework is."
  5. A line of dialogue"'You'll never finish it in time,' my brother said. He was almost right."

Match the opening to the text type: a scene suits an article or story; a bold claim suits a letter or speech; a proposal or report should open with its purpose.

Linking ideas

"Firstly, secondly, lastly" shows you have a list. Better linking shows how the ideas are related.

Link with ideas, not just words

The strongest writers link by picking up a word from the last sentence:

  • "Many students sleep less than six hours a night. This lack of sleep shows in their concentration."

The goal isn't longer words. It's words that say exactly what you mean.

  • Using a word you found in a thesaurus but have never seen in a sentence.

Sentence variety

At the top levels, examiners look for control: short sentences for impact, long ones for detail, and you choosing which.

The same three facts, three ways

Tap a levelClick a level or use ← → keys

Level 3

The school has a new rule. Students cannot use phones. Many students are unhappy about it.

Three sentences of the same shape. Correct, but flat.

Level 5

Since the school banned phones last month, many students have been unhappy, arguing that they need them to check homework updates.

One complex sentence connects cause, result and reason.

Level 5**

The phone ban arrived quietly, in a one-line notice on the school website. The reaction was anything but quiet.

A long sentence sets the scene; a short one lands the punch. The contrast between "quietly" and "anything but quiet" makes the reader smile.

Structures to try this week

  • Not only did …, but it also …
  • What students need is …
  • Had the school acted earlier, …
  • The more we …, the less …
  • Only when … do we realise …
  • It is not …, but … that …

Use one or two of these in a piece. Five in a row sounds forced.
